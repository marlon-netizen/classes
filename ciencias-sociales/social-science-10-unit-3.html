Lesson 1 of 4

Nicaragua during the independence process

Nicaragua during the independence process: the end of Spanish rule

For about three centuries Nicaragua belonged to the Spanish Empire as a province of the Captaincy General of Guatemala, with its capital in León. Between 1811 and 1838 the country moved from colony to independent state. This lesson looks at what Nicaragua was like in that process (political, social and economic characteristics), who led it (the próceres), and how it changed the economy.

1.1 Political, social and economic characteristics

Political

Top offices were held by peninsulares (Spain-born officials); criollos were excluded from the highest posts. León (administrative and Church center) and Granada (merchant city on Lake Nicaragua) became rivals, a rivalry that outlived the colony.

Social

A hierarchy of origin and caste: peninsulares, criollos, mestizos, Indigenous communities (who paid tribute) and Afro-descendants, including enslaved people. Slavery was abolished by the Central American Federation in 1824.

Economic

Agriculture and cattle (cacao, indigo, hides) plus timber. Trade went through Spain's monopoly, with smuggling on the Caribbean coast. Coins were scarce and the economy was small and regional.

Ideas and events

Enlightenment ideas, the 1812 Cádiz Constitution and the collapse of Spanish authority after Napoleon's invasion (1808) encouraged criollo elites to demand more power.

From colony to sovereign state

1811–1812 uprisings in Granada, León, Masaya and other towns→15 Sept 1821: independence declared in Guatemala→1822–1823: annexed to Iturbide's Mexican Empire→1823–1838: United Provinces of Central America→1838: Nicaragua declares itself sovereign, free and independent

Nicaraguan leaders were divided about the path: some wanted union with Mexico, others a Central American union, others a state of their own. The disagreement fed civil wars in the 1820s.

1.2 Próceres of the independence

A prócer is a leading figure of a nation's independence. Use these cards as the start of your "historical album" (check dates and details in your textbook).

Miguel Larreynaga

Jurist, scholar and educator from León. He represented Nicaragua when the Act of Independence was signed in Guatemala (1821) and later served the new state in its institutions.

Cleto Ordóñez

Military officer from Granada, remembered for his role in the 1811–1812 uprisings against Spanish authority.

Telésforo Orozco and the Granada patriots

Leaders of the first rebellions of 1811 who demanded changes in colonial government and trade.

Manuel Antonio de la Cerda

First Head of State of Nicaragua within the Federation (1825). He died in the civil wars that followed, a sign of how unstable the new state was.
Older roots of resistance: In 1762 the young Rafaela Herrera helped defend the fortress of the Immaculate Conception on the San Juan River against a British attack. Defending the homeland did not begin with independence.

Lesson 2 of 4

The formation of the Nicaraguan State

The formation of the Nicaraguan State

Independence did not create a strong state overnight. From 1838 to the 1890s Nicaragua built its institutions amid rivalry between elites, foreign interests and war. In this lesson you will study the characteristics of that process, its socioeconomic consequences, and the legacy of the heroes who defended national sovereignty and identity.

2.1 Political and economic characteristics

Political

Constitutions and governments changed often. Liberals (centered in León) and Conservatives (centered in Granada) struggled for power. Managua became the capital in 1852 as a compromise between the two cities. After 1857 Conservative governments ruled for about thirty years.

Economic

An agro-export economy based on the primary sector: first indigo and cattle, then coffee from the mid-1800s. Estates (haciendas) and exports were controlled by a small elite.

Foreign interest

Britain held influence on the Caribbean coast (the Mosquito Reserve) and the United States sought a canal route. In 1851–1856 the Transit route (San Juan River and Lake Nicaragua) carried travelers heading to California.

Institutions

Congresses, courts, an army and tax systems were slowly built, often disrupted by civil war.

Graphic organizer: from export to export

Colonial: cacao, indigo, cattle→Early republic: indigo and cattle, local trade→From the 1850s: coffee in the Pacific and central highlands→Land ownership becomes the basis of political power

Owning land meant controlling workers, harvests and local politics. That is why land tenure became the base of the elites' political power.

2.2 Socioeconomic consequences

  • Concentration of land: coffee growers acquired land, often taking communal lands of Indigenous and peasant communities.
  • Forced labor and resistance: in 1881 Indigenous communities in Matagalpa rose up against forced labor and the loss of their land.
  • Dependence: the economy relied on one main export and on foreign markets and merchants.
  • Instability and interference: the elites' rivalry opened the door to foreign adventurers, as the next section shows.

2.3 The National War (1856–1857) and the legacy of heroes

In 1855 a Liberal faction in León invited the American adventurer William Walker to help in a civil war. Walker and his filibusters took Granada, and in 1856 he declared himself president and restored slavery. Conservatives, Liberals and the other Central American countries united against him.

1855: Walker arrives→11 Apr 1856: Battle of Rivas→14 Sept 1856: Battle of San Jacinto→Dec 1856: Granada burned by the filibusters→1 May 1857: Walker surrenders at Rivas

José Dolores Estrada

Commander of the Nicaraguan force that defeated Walker's filibusters at the hacienda San Jacinto (14 September 1856). He is a national hero.

Andrés Castro

A soldier at San Jacinto. Tradition says that when his ammunition ran out he fought with a stone. He became a symbol of ordinary Nicaraguans defending the nation.
Beyond soldiers: Estrada and Castro are not remembered only as military men but as defenders of the idea of Nicaragua as a sovereign state against foreign threats.

Lesson 3 of 4

Anti-interventionist policies, 1893–1909

Anti-interventionist policies in Nicaragua, 1893–1909

José Santos Zelaya (born in Managua, 1853) led the Liberal Revolution of 1893 and governed Nicaragua until 1909. His government is one of the most debated in Nicaraguan history: admired for defending national sovereignty and modernizing the country, criticized for concentrating power.

3.1 Political, social and economic characteristics

Political

A new Liberal constitution (1893, nicknamed La Libérrima) separated Church and State and recognized civil liberties. Power became highly centralized in the president.

Social

Expansion of public secular education, civil marriage and civil registry. Opponents and some press suffered restrictions.

Economic

Coffee exports financed railways, telegraph lines, ports, roads and a stronger state. Foreign companies were active on the Caribbean coast.

International

Zelaya resisted foreign pressure, especially from the United States, over canal rights and the control of the country's resources.

Action and consequence

Policy or characteristicConsequenceHow it defended sovereignty
Reincorporation of the Mosquito Reserve (1894)The Caribbean coast, under British-protected autonomy, came under full Nicaraguan law (the Department of Zelaya).Established national control over the whole territory. (Debate: Indigenous and Creole communities lost the self-government of the Reserve.)
Refusing humiliating canal termsTension with the United States, which preferred a canal under its own control.Defended Nicaragua's right to decide about its own resources and territory.
Modernization with national revenueRailways, telegraph, ports and schools expanded.Reduced reliance on foreign powers and strengthened the national State.
Liberal constitution and educationSecular State, wider civil rights, more public schools.Built a modern, national citizenship.
Central American union projects (1895–1898)Short-lived "Greater Republic of Central America."Sought regional strength against outside powers. (Critics: it also meddled in neighbors' affairs.)

The 1909 crisis and the Knox Note

Oct 1909: Conservative revolt starts in Bluefields→Nov 1909: two Americans who joined the rebels are executed→Dec 1909: U.S. Secretary of State Knox sends the Knox Note→Dec 1909: Zelaya resigns
  • The Knox Note (December 1909) condemned Zelaya's government and broke diplomatic relations. It also implied that the rebels represented the Nicaraguan people better than the government did.
  • What the United States wanted: a government friendly to its commercial and strategic interests and, above all, control over a possible canal route.
  • Why it violated sovereignty: one country decided which government was legitimate for another, ignoring Nicaragua's right to self-determination.
  • In 1903 the U.S. had already chosen Panama for its canal. Nicaragua's route remained an alternative, and Zelaya refused to grant it on terms he saw as humiliating.

3.2 The legacy of Zelaya

Arguments for his legacy

Defended national sovereignty, reincorporated the Caribbean coast, modernized the state and economy, and refused to hand over the canal under humiliating conditions.

Arguments of critics

Ruled as a strongman, limited opposition and press, used monopolies and forced loans, and also interfered in neighboring countries.

Guide for a critical essay

  1. Introduction: state your thesis (for example, "Zelaya's policies defended Nicaraguan sovereignty even though his rule was authoritarian").
  2. Argument 1: how the U.S. intervened (canal, support for rebels, the Knox Note).
  3. Argument 2: why Zelaya did not give the canal under humiliating conditions.
  4. Counterargument: criticisms of his government, and your answer to them.
  5. Conclusion: your nationalist, evidence-based position.
Nicaraguan connection: Zelaya's name remains on the Caribbean coast, where the former Department of Zelaya covered much of today's RACCN and RACCS. The debate between sovereignty and autonomy is still alive in the region.
Your teacher will look for (checklist): (1) you identify precisely Zelaya's economic reforms and policies; (2) you argue about nationalism, the defense of sovereignty and Nicaragua's position toward foreign powers.

Lesson 4 of 4

Struggles for liberation against the U.S. interventions, 1910–1912

Struggles for liberation against the U.S. interventions, 1910–1912

After Zelaya's fall the country entered a period of dependence and war. In 1912 General Benjamín Zeledón (1879–1912) led the resistance against the government of Adolfo Díaz and the U.S. Marines. His last stand, his final letter and his death made him a National Hero of Nicaragua.

4.1 Historical, political and socio-geographic context

Dec 1909: Zelaya resigns→1910: U.S. Marines land at Bluefields; Madriz resigns→1910–1911: Conservative governments with U.S. backing→1911: Adolfo Díaz becomes president→1912: Revolt of General Mena; Díaz asks for U.S. troops→Aug 1912: Marines land→Oct 1912: Coyotepe and La Barranca
  • Political context: the governments after 1909 depended on U.S. support, money and arms. Díaz, who had worked for a U.S.-owned mining company, asked for the Marines when his government was threatened.
  • Historical context: U.S. expansion in the Caribbean and Central America, interest in the canal route, and loans controlled by foreign banks.
  • What came after: the Marines stayed in Nicaragua for years, and in 1914 the Bryan–Chamorro Treaty gave the U.S. exclusive canal rights. That is the background of Sandino's struggle in the 1920s.

Socio-geographic context: Masaya

Where?

The department of Masaya, in the Pacific region, between Managua and Granada, a densely populated area of towns, peasants and artisans.

The hills

Coyotepe and La Barranca are hills above Masaya. They overlook the city and the road and railway between the capital and Granada.

Why they mattered

Whoever held them controlled the passage toward the capital and had a strong view of the attackers.

Relief and resistance

The steep slopes favored the defenders against a better-armed force, though Marine artillery and infantry finally prevailed.

4.2 The heroic legacy of Benjamín Zeledón

  • The man: a lawyer, born in Managua, and a Liberal general. Liberal ideas, respect for international law and a deep patriotism shaped him.
  • The resistance: in 1912 he led the opposition to the Díaz government and to foreign troops. On 3–4 October 1912 his forces defended Coyotepe and La Barranca.
  • His death: he was captured and executed on 4 October 1912 (in the area of Niquinohomo and Catarina) and was buried in Catarina.
  • The last letter to his wife, Esther: expresses patriotism, sacrifice and honor. He chose not to surrender even though he knew he would probably die.
Why he matters: he stood for the principle that sovereignty is not negotiable. He preferred sacrifice to bargaining away the independence of the country.

Díaz government and U.S. forces

Held power with foreign military support; saw intervention as a way to keep order.

Zeledón's resistance

Defended national self-determination against a government sustained by foreign troops.

Impact on Sandino

The young Augusto Nicolás Calderón Sandino (born in 1895 in Niquinohomo) saw Zeledón's body in 1912. That memory became the seed of the struggle for national liberation that Sandino would lead years later, in the Army in Defense of National Sovereignty.

Guide for a classroom conversation

  • Which values appear in Zeledón's last letter (patriotism, sacrifice, honor)? Quote or paraphrase.
  • Why did he decide not to surrender, knowing he would likely die?
  • Before you answer a classmate, paraphrase what they said: "If I understand you, you think that…"
Nicaraguan connection: Coyotepe is now a historic fortress near Masaya and the town of Catarina keeps Zeledón's tomb. His anniversary, 4 October, is a day of remembrance.
Your teacher will look for (checklist): (1) you explain the struggles against the U.S. interventions of 1910–1912; (2) you describe the socio-geographic context of the resistance (Coyotepe and La Barranca); (3) you recognize Zeledón's heroic legacy in the defense of sovereignty.
