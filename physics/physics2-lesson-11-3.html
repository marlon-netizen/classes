Part A

Wave motion

Drop a pebble into a pond and ripples travel outward from the disturbance. A leaf floating near the disturbance moves up and down and back and forth about its original position, but it is not carried along by the waves. The water molecules move locally, like the leaf, but they do not travel across the pond: the wave moves from one place to another, the water itself is not carried with it.

MediumThe physical environment through which a disturbance can travel (the water of the pond). The medium does not travel with the wave; after the wave has passed, it returns to its original position.
Mechanical waveA wave that requires a medium through which to travel (water waves, sound). Sound cannot travel through the near-vacuum of space. Electromagnetic waves (light, radio, microwaves, x-rays) can travel through a vacuum.

A wave is the motion of a disturbance. A single flip of a rope creates a pulse wave; repeated pulses from a periodic motion such as a hand moving up and down form a periodic wave. If the source vibrates with simple harmonic motion, the result is a sine wave: every point of the medium vibrates with simple harmonic motion, so the terms from Lesson 11.2 (amplitude, period, frequency) apply to waves too.

Part B

Wave types and waveforms

Transverse waveThe particles of the medium move perpendicularly to the direction the wave travels (a wave on a rope). Crests are the highest points above the equilibrium position, troughs the lowest points below it.
Longitudinal waveThe particles move parallel to the direction the wave travels (sound in air, a pushed-and-pulled spring). Compressed regions are the crests of the waveform; stretched regions are the troughs: a density wave or pressure wave.

A waveform is the graph of a wave. For a transverse wave at one instant, the x-axis is the equilibrium position and the y-coordinates are the displacements. Where the curve crosses the axis the displacement is zero; at crests and troughs it is greatest.

  • Amplitude: the distance from the equilibrium position to a crest or to a trough.
  • Wavelength, λ (lambda): the distance the wave travels during one cycle: the distance between two adjacent similar points, crest to crest or trough to trough. Unit: metre (m).
  • A waveform can show the displacement of every point at a single moment in time, or the displacement of a single particle as time passes.

Part C

Period, frequency and wave speed

The vibrating source (a vocal cord, a guitar string, a drumhead) has a characteristic frequency, and the particles of the medium vibrate at that same frequency. When the particles complete one cycle, one complete wavelength passes any given point: so the period of a wave is the time for one wavelength to pass a point, and the frequency is the number of wavelengths passing per second. As before, f = 1/T.

Speed is displacement divided by time. In one period the wave travels one wavelength, so:

v = λT = fλ
Speed of a wave
v = fλ

speed of a wave = frequency × wavelength

The speed of a mechanical wave is constant for any given medium. At a concert, sounds of different frequencies reach your ears at the same moment. When the frequency of a wave increases, its wavelength must decrease so that the product fλ (the speed) stays the same. The speed changes only when the wave moves into another medium, or when properties of the medium (such as temperature) change.

Did you know?The frequencies of sound waves that are audible to humans range from 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Electromagnetic waves, which include visible light, radio waves and microwaves, have an even broader range of frequencies: from about 104 Hz to 1025 Hz. All electromagnetic waves travel at 3.00 × 108 m/s in empty space.
Part D

Waves transfer energy

The water wave from a pebble carries energy from one place in the pond to another while the water stays in essentially the same place: a wave transfers energy by the vibration of matter, not by the transfer of matter itself.

The rate at which a wave transfers energy depends on its amplitude. A greater amplitude means greater compression and stretching, so more potential energy in each cycle. For a mechanical wave, the energy transferred is proportional to the square of the amplitude: doubling the amplitude gives four times the energy in a given time; halving the amplitude gives one quarter. Over time the amplitude gradually decreases as the energy is dissipated (damping); we ignore damping in our examples.
