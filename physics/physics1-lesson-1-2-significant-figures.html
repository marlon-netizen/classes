Part A

Rules for counting significant figures

Every measurement is limited by the instrument that made it. When you read a measuring tool, you record every digit you are certain of plus one estimated digit. Those digits together are the significant figures (sig figs) — they show how well you know the value.

ExampleA ruler marked in millimeters shows a pencil ending between 12.3 cm and 12.4 cm. You estimate 12.34 cm: the digits 1, 2, 3 are certain and the 4 is estimated. That is 4 significant figures.
  1. Nonzero digits are always significant.
    456 → 3 sig figs  ·  2.718 → 4 sig figs
  2. Zeros between nonzero digits (“captive” zeros) are significant.
    105 → 3  ·  4.007 → 4
  3. Leading zeros are never significant. They only show where the decimal point is.
    0.0052 → 2  ·  0.00047 → 2
  4. Trailing zeros are significant only if the number has a decimal point.
    2.50 → 3  ·  100. → 3  ·  0.0120 → 3  ·  but 1200 → 2 and 300 → 1
  5. In scientific notation, every digit in the coefficient is significant. It is the best way to show exactly how many sig figs you mean.
    4.50 × 103 → 3  ·  4.5 × 103 → 2  ·  6.02 × 1023 → 3
  6. Exact numbers have unlimited sig figs. Counted values (12 eggs) and definitions (100 cm = 1 m) never limit your answer.
    “4 sides” · “2 × radius” · 60 s = 1 min

Part B

Precision vs. accuracy

The two words are often used as if they mean the same thing, but in science they do not.

Accuracy

How close a measurement is to the true (accepted) value.

Affected by systematic error — a consistent bias, such as a scale that was never zeroed.

Precision

How close repeated measurements are to each other (how reproducible they are). It is also limited by how finely the instrument is divided.

Affected by random error — unpredictable scatter, such as reaction time.

Example with data

Four students measure the free-fall acceleration. The accepted value is 9.81 m/s². Each did three trials.

Here “accurate” means the average is within 0.1 m/s² of 9.81, and “precise” means the three trials are within 0.1 m/s² of each other.

Percent error
percent error = | measured − accepted |accepted × 100%

A small percent error means a measurement is accurate. Example: measured 9.50 m/s², accepted 9.81 m/s² → |−0.31| ÷ 9.81 × 100% = 3.2%.

Don’t mix them up

Sig figs communicate precision (how finely you measured), not accuracy. A ruler with millimeter marks gives a more precise measurement than one with centimeter marks — but if the ruler is stretched, both can be inaccurate.

Part C

Rules for sig figs in calculations

A calculated answer cannot be more certain than the measurements it came from. The rule you use depends on the operation.

Multiply & divide

Keep as many significant figures as the measurement with the fewest sig figs.

4.56 × 1.4 = 6.384 → 6.4

1.4 has 2 sig figs, so the answer has 2.

Add & subtract

Keep as many decimal places as the measurement with the fewest decimal places (the least precise place value).

12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013 = 31.123 → 31.1

18.0 only goes to the tenths place, so the answer does too.

Rounding

  • Look at the digit just to the right of the last digit you keep. If it is 0–4, round down (leave the digit). If it is 5–9, round up.
  • Round only at the end. Keep one or two extra (“guard”) digits in the middle of a multi-step problem, then round the final answer.
  • Exact numbers (counts, definitions) don’t limit the answer.
  • Use scientific notation when rounding leaves ambiguous trailing zeros: 1 234 rounded to 2 sig figs is 1.2 × 103, not “1200”.
Mixed operations

Follow the order of operations. Apply the correct rule at each step but carry a guard digit, then round the final result by the rule for the last operation.
