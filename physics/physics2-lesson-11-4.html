Part A

Wave interference

Two bumper boats cannot occupy the same place at the same time: they bounce apart. Waves behave differently. Mechanical waves are not matter but displacements of matter, so two waves can pass through the same space at the same time and then continue unchanged. You can hear a trumpet and a flute together because their sound waves pass through each other without destroying each other.

The combination of two overlapping waves is called superposition. In a ripple tank, two sets of water waves pass through one another and form an interference pattern of light and dark bands.

Superposition principleWhen two or more waves travel through a medium at the same time, the resultant wave is the sum of the displacements of the individual waves at each point. (It holds when the amplitudes are small, which is our assumption in every example.)
yresultant(x) = y1(x) + y2(x)

After two pulses pass through each other, each has the same shape it had before and keeps traveling in the same direction.

Part B

Constructive and destructive interference

Constructive interferenceThe displacements are on the same side of the equilibrium position (same sign). They add, so the resultant is larger than either one. When two pulses coincide exactly, the amplitude is the sum of the amplitudes: A = A1 + A2.
Destructive interferenceThe displacements are on opposite sides (opposite signs). The resultant is the difference: |A1 − A2|. If the amplitudes are equal, the pulses cancel completely at that instant: complete destructive interference (resultant = 0).

Left: two pulses on the same side coincide (constructive: amplitude 0.50 m). Right: pulses on opposite sides (destructive: 0.10 m).

The superposition principle is valid for longitudinal waves too. In a compression the particles are closer together; in a rarefaction they are farther apart. When a compression and a rarefaction interfere, the interference is destructive.

RememberAt the instant of complete destructive interference the string is flat, but the pulses have not disappeared: after they pass each other, they continue with their original shapes.
Part C

Reflection

What happens when a wave reaches a boundary? It is reflected.

Free boundaryThe end of the rope is a ring that slides without friction on a post. The reflected pulse is upright (not inverted) and has the same amplitude as the incident pulse.
Fixed boundaryThe rope is tied to a wall. The rope pulls up on the wall; the wall pulls down on the rope (Newton’s third law). The reflected pulse is inverted, with the same amplitude.

A pulse reaching the end of a rope: with a fixed end the reflection is inverted; with a free end it is upright.

TipWhen the reflected pulse crosses the incident pulse: at a fixed end they have opposite signs (destructive); at a free end they have the same sign (constructive: amplitudes add).
Part D

Standing waves

Shake one end of a string that is attached to a rigid support. The waves travel down the string, are reflected at the other end and travel back. If the string is vibrated at exactly the right frequency, the incoming and reflected waves (same frequency, wavelength and amplitude, opposite directions) form a standing wave: a resultant pattern that appears to stay in place, with alternating regions of constructive and destructive interference.

NodeA point of a standing wave that maintains zero displacement (complete destructive interference). The string does not move there.
AntinodeA point halfway between two nodes where the largest displacement occurs (the largest amplitude).

The first four standing waves on a string of length L with both ends fixed. N = node, A = antinode.

Which wavelengths make a standing wave?

The ends of the string are fixed, so both ends must be nodes. A single loop is half a wavelength. A standing wave exists only when the string length holds a whole number of loops:

L = n (λ ⁄ 2)  →  λ = 2L ⁄ n    (n = 1, 2, 3, …)

Wavelengths between these values do not produce standing waves, because only one end (or neither) could be a node. Since v = fλ, each allowed wavelength corresponds to an allowed frequency f = v ⁄ λ.

Remember when solving problems

  1. Analyze — draw the waves; note the sign (side of equilibrium) of each displacement; is the boundary fixed or free?
  2. Plan — add displacements point by point (superposition); for standing waves use L = nλ/2.
  3. Solve — same side: add the amplitudes; opposite sides: subtract them; fixed end: invert on reflection.
  4. Check — the resultant is never larger than the sum of the amplitudes; both ends of a fixed string are nodes.
