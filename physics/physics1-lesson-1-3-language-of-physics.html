The Language of Physics

Chapter 1, Section 3. Physicists build simplified models of the real world, then use mathematics to analyze and summarize observations. The mathematical relationships among physical quantities let us predict what will happen in new situations.

Objectives:

  • Interpret data in tables and graphs, and recognize equations that summarize data.
  • Distinguish between conventions for abbreviating units and quantities.
  • Use dimensional analysis to check the validity of equations.
  • Perform order-of-magnitude calculations.

1. Tables, graphs and equations summarize data

In a famous experiment testing Galileo’s hypothesis that all objects fall at the same rate without air resistance, a table-tennis ball and a golf ball are dropped in a vacuum. The data are organized in a table:

A graph of distance versus time shows an obvious pattern (a curve), so we can draw a smooth curve through the points to estimate values at times where we have no data. The data can also be summarized by an equation:

(change in position in meters) = 4.9 × (time of fall in seconds)² In symbols: Δy = 4.9(Δt)²

This equation reproduces the graph and predicts the change in position at any time during the fall.

2. Symbols and conventions

  • Letters stand for quantities: for example, v is speed.
  • The Greek letter Δ (delta) means “difference or change in”. The Greek letter Σ (sigma) means “sum” or “total”.
  • Variables and quantities are written in italic or boldface letters. Units are written in regular (roman) letters.

3. Proportional relationships

  • Directly proportional: if one variable changes by a factor, the other changes by the same factor.
  • Inversely proportional: if one variable changes by a factor, the other changes by the inverse of that factor.
Force = mass × acceleration

Doubling the force doubles the acceleration (same mass): force and acceleration are directly proportional. If the force stays constant, doubling the mass cuts the acceleration in half: mass and acceleration are inversely proportional.

4. Dimensional analysis weeds out invalid equations

Dimensions can be treated like algebraic quantities. Quantities can be added or subtracted only if they have the same dimensions, and both sides of an equation must have the same dimensions.

Use it before computing: checking the dimensions of your equation prevents wasting time on an invalid one.

5. Order-of-magnitude estimates check answers

An order-of-magnitude calculation finds the power of 10 closest to the actual value. It lets you judge whether a more exact answer is reasonable, and it can estimate quantities when little information is given.

Worked examples

Example 1 — Using the equation Δy = 4.9(Δt)²

How far does a ball fall in 0.200 s? Compare it with the table.

Δy = 4.9 × (0.200)² = 4.9 × 0.0400 = 0.196 m = 19.6 cm

The table gives 19.60 cm at 0.200 s. ✓

Example 2 — Dimensional analysis

A car moves at 88 km/h. How long does it take to travel 725 km? Which operation gives a result with the dimension of time?

Try multiplying: lengthtime × length = length²time. This is not a time, so it is invalid.

Try dividing: lengthlength/time = length × timelength = time. ✓

Δt = 725 km88 km/h = 725 km × 1.0 h88 km ≈ 8.2 h

Example 3 — Order of magnitude

Estimate the time for the same trip.

725 km is closer to 10³ km (1000 km) than to 10² km, and 88 km/h is about 10² km/h.

10³ km10² km/h = 10 h

The answer should be closer to 10 than to 1 or 100. The exact answer, 8.2 h, fits. ✓

Example 4 — Estimating with little information

About how many gallons of gasoline do all cars in the United States use each year?

Assume 300 million people, 5 people per family and 2 cars per family: about 120 million cars (12 × 10&sup7;).

Assume 10 000 mi (10⁴ mi) per car per year at 20 mi/gal: 10 000 mi/yr × 1 gal20 mi = 500 gal/yr per car.

Total: (12 × 10&sup7; cars)(500 gal/car) = 6 × 10¹⁰ gal per year
