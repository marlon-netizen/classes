Part A

Amplitude, period and frequency

In the absence of friction, a moving trapeze always returns to the same maximum displacement after each swing. That maximum displacement from the equilibrium position is the amplitude. A pendulum's amplitude is measured by the angle between its equilibrium position and its maximum displacement; for a mass-spring system it is the greatest stretch or compression of the spring.

Imagine a ride swinging from maximum displacement on one side of equilibrium to maximum displacement on the other side and back again: that is one complete cycle.

Period, TThe time it takes to complete one full cycle. Unit: second (s). After a time T the object is back where it started.
Frequency, fThe number of cycles (vibrations) per unit of time. Unit: hertz (Hz = s−1).

Period and frequency are both about time, which makes them easy to confuse. The period is time per cycle; the frequency is cycles per time, so they are inverses of each other:

f = 1T   or   T = 1f

Example: if one complete cycle takes 20 s, then f = 1/20 s = 0.05 Hz. In any problem where you know one of them, you can calculate the other.

Part B

The period of a simple pendulum

A simple pendulum and a mass-spring system both vibrate with simple harmonic motion, but each needs its own equation because the period depends on different physical factors. For a pendulum, the length is measured from the pivot to the centre of mass of the bob. Pull two pendulums of the same length but different bob masses aside by the same small distance and release them together: they complete each vibration in the same time. Change the amplitude of one (for small angles) and the periods are still equal. For small amplitudes the period of a pendulum does not depend on the mass or on the amplitude. It does depend on the length and on the free-fall acceleration.

Period of a simple pendulum in simple harmonic motion
T = 2π Lag

period = 2π × square root of (length divided by free-fall acceleration). Use ag = 9.81 m/s² on Earth's surface unless the problem gives another value.

Why? A shorter pendulum has a smaller arc to travel while the acceleration from the restoring force stays the same, so it has a shorter period. Mass does not matter for the same reason free fall does not depend on mass: a heavier bob feels a bigger restoring force but also needs a bigger force to reach the same acceleration. For small angles (less than 15°) a larger amplitude gives a larger restoring force and a longer path, and the two effects cancel.

Part C

The period of a mass-spring system

In a mass-spring system Hooke's law gives the restoring force, Felastic = −kx, so the force depends on the displacement and the spring constant, not on the mass. A heavier mass therefore adds inertia without adding force: it accelerates less and takes more time to complete a cycle. So a larger mass means a longer period. A larger spring constant k (a stiffer spring) means a larger force for the same stretch, a larger acceleration and a shorter period.

Period of a mass-spring system in simple harmonic motion
T = 2π mk

period = 2π × square root of (mass divided by spring constant). Changing the amplitude does not change the period – this holds only while the spring obeys Hooke's law.
