The Addition Rule (Probability)

The addition rule helps us find the probability that event A or event B (or both) occurs. We add the two probabilities, but since outcomes in both events get counted twice, we subtract the overlap once.

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) Also written: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

Special case: mutually exclusive events

Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time, so P(A and B) = 0. The rule simplifies to:

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)

Tip: “or” in probability is inclusive — it means A, B, or both.

Useful reminders

  • All probabilities are between 0 and 1.
  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • P(neither A nor B) = 1 − P(A or B).
  • Rearranged: P(A and B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A or B).

Worked examples

Example 1 — Overlapping events (cards)

A card is drawn from a standard 52-card deck. Find the probability that it is a king or a heart.

P(king) = 452, P(heart) = 1352, P(king of hearts) = 152

P(king or heart) = 452 + 1352 − 152 = 1652 = 413 ≈ 0.308

Example 2 — Mutually exclusive events (dice)

A fair die is rolled. Find P(roll a 2 or roll a 5).

A roll cannot be both 2 and 5, so the events are mutually exclusive.

P = 16 + 16 = 13 ≈ 0.333

Example 3 — Using a two-way table

In a class of 40 students, the table shows who owns a bike and/or a skateboard. Find P(bike or skateboard) for a randomly chosen student.

SkateboardNo skateboardTotal
Bike81725
No bike6915
Total142640

P(bike) = 25/40, P(skateboard) = 14/40, P(both) = 8/40

P(bike or skateboard) = (25 + 14 − 8)/40 = 31/40 = 0.775

Check: 1 − P(neither) = 1 − 9/40 = 31/40 ✓

Example 4 — Finding a missing value

P(A) = 0.50, P(B) = 0.30 and P(A or B) = 0.65. Find P(A and B).

0.65 = 0.50 + 0.30 − P(A and B)

P(A and B) = 0.80 − 0.65 = 0.15
