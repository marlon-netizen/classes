From a sample to a population proportion

Unit 3 asks one big question: what can a sample proportion tell us about the population proportion? In these four lessons you will learn to (3.1) judge estimators, (3.2) describe the sampling distribution of a sample proportion, (3.3) build a confidence interval for a population proportion, and (3.4) interpret it and use it to justify claims.

p = number of successesn The sample proportion p (a statistic) estimates the population proportion p (a parameter).

Reference: Barron’s AP Statistics — the chapters on sampling distributions and on confidence intervals for proportions. Barron’s is organized differently from our course sequence, so use the topics, not the chapter order, to find the matching sections. Notation and the four-step write-up (State, Plan, Do, Conclude) follow AP exam conventions.

LESSON 3.1

Estimators

  • A parameter is a number that describes a population (e.g. p, μ). It is usually unknown.
  • A statistic is a number computed from a sample (e.g. p, x). It varies from sample to sample.
  • A point estimator is a statistic used to estimate a parameter; its value for one sample is a point estimate. The sample proportion p is a point estimator of p.
  • An estimator is unbiased if the mean of its sampling distribution equals the parameter it estimates. Individual estimates still miss the target — “unbiased” means no systematic over- or underestimating.
Unbiased:   μstatistic = parameter Unbiased: p for p, sample mean for μ, sample variance s² for σ². A statistic whose sampling distribution is centered above or below the parameter is biased.
Remember: bias is about the center of the sampling distribution. A larger sample size does not remove bias; it reduces variability.
LESSON 3.2

Sampling distribution of a sample proportion

If we took every possible random sample of size n from a population with proportion p, the values of p form the sampling distribution of p.

μp = p      σp = √p(1 − p) / n The formula for σ requires independent observations.

Conditions for using these formulas and a Normal model:

  • Random: data come from a random sample or randomized experiment.
  • 10% condition (independence when sampling without replacement): n ≤ 0.10N.
  • Large counts (approximately Normal): np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10.

When the conditions hold, p is approximately Normal, so we find probabilities with z-scores:

z = p − pσp

Interpretation: the standard deviation σp is the typical distance between a sample proportion and the true p in repeated random samples of that size. Because σp has √n in the denominator, quadrupling n cuts it in half. See it happen in the Simulation tab.

LESSON 3.3

Constructing a confidence interval for a population proportion

The procedure is the one-sample z-interval for a population proportion:

p ± z* · √p(1 − p) / n point estimate ± margin of error  |  ME = z* × SE  |  SE = √(p(1 − p)/n)

Four-step write-up:

  1. State: define the parameter in context (“p = the true proportion of … in …”) and the confidence level.
  2. Plan: name the procedure (one-sample z-interval for p) and verify conditions: random sample; 10% (n ≤ 0.10N); large counts np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10.
  3. Do: compute p, the standard error, the margin of error and the interval.
  4. Conclude: interpret the interval in context (see 3.4).

Sample size for a desired margin of error (solve ME = z*√(p(1 − p)/n) for n):

n ≥ (z*ME)2 · p(1 − p) If there is no prior estimate, use p = 0.5 (the most conservative value). Always round n up.
LESSON 3.4

Justifying a claim based on a confidence interval

  • Interpreting the interval: “We are C% confident that the interval from a to b captures the true proportion of [population in context].”
  • Interpreting the confidence level: if we repeatedly took random samples of the same size and built an interval from each, about C% of those intervals would capture the true proportion.
  • Common errors: “There is a 95% probability that p is in (a, b)” (the parameter is fixed; the interval is what varies), or “95% of the population/sample is in the interval.”
  • Justifying a claim: if a claimed value of p is inside the interval, it is a plausible value (no convincing evidence against it). If it is outside, the interval provides convincing evidence that the claim is not true. An interval entirely above 0.5 supports “more than half”.
  • Why can an interval miss p? Random sampling variability: a 95% interval fails to capture p in about 5% of samples.

Worked examples

Example 1 (3.1) — Parameter, statistic and unbiasedness

A random sample of 250 students from a school of 2000 is asked about online textbooks: 90 prefer them. (a) Identify the parameter and the statistic. (b) A simulation of 1000 samples of size 250 from a population with p = 0.35 gives sample proportions centered at 0.351. What does that suggest?

(a) Parameter: p = proportion of all 2000 students who prefer online textbooks (unknown). Statistic: p = 90/250 = 0.36, our point estimate.

(b) The mean of the simulated sampling distribution (0.351) is essentially equal to p = 0.35, which supports that p is an unbiased estimator of p.

Example 2 (3.2) — Mean, SD and a probability

40% of all fans at a stadium prefer the home-team jersey. A random sample of n = 150 fans (far fewer than 10% of the stadium) is taken. Describe the sampling distribution of p and find P(p > 0.45).

Conditions: random sample; 10% condition holds; np = 150(0.40) = 60 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) = 90 ≥ 10, so p is approximately Normal.

μp = 0.40    σp = √(0.40 · 0.60/150) = √0.0016 = 0.04

z = (0.45 − 0.40)/0.04 = 1.25, so P(p > 0.45) = 1 − 0.8944 = 0.1056

Interpretation: about 10.6% of random samples of 150 fans would have more than 45% preferring the home jersey.

Example 3 (3.3) — A 95% confidence interval

In a random sample of 400 voters (from a city of 90 000), 232 favor a proposal. Construct and interpret a 95% confidence interval for the proportion of all voters who favor it.

State: p = the true proportion of all voters in the city who favor the proposal. 95% confidence.

Plan: one-sample z-interval for p. Random sample ✓; 400 < 10% of 90 000 ✓; 400(0.58) = 232 ≥ 10 and 400(0.42) = 168 ≥ 10 ✓.

Do: p = 232/400 = 0.58; SE = √(0.58 · 0.42/400) ≈ 0.0247; ME = 1.96(0.0247) ≈ 0.0484.

Interval: 0.58 ± 0.0484 = (0.532, 0.628)

Conclude: We are 95% confident that the interval from 0.532 to 0.628 captures the true proportion of all voters in the city who favor the proposal.

Example 4 (3.3) — Sample size for a given margin of error

A pollster wants a 95% confidence interval with a margin of error of at most 0.03 and has no prior estimate of p. How many people should be sampled?

n ≥ (1.96/0.03)²(0.5)(0.5) = 4268.4 × 0.25 ≈ 1067.1

Round up: n = 1068.

Example 5 (3.4) — Justifying a claim

A 95% confidence interval for the proportion of teens who sleep fewer than 8 hours on school nights is (0.58, 0.66). (a) Is there convincing evidence that a majority of teens sleep fewer than 8 hours? (b) A website claims the proportion is 0.70. What does the interval say?

(a) All plausible values (0.58 to 0.66) are above 0.50, so the interval provides convincing evidence that more than half of teens sleep fewer than 8 hours.

(b) 0.70 is not in the interval, so the interval gives convincing evidence that the true proportion is not 0.70.

Example 6 (3.4) — What happens to the margin of error?

A survey with n = 400 has a margin of error of 0.05 at 95% confidence. What margin of error would you expect with n = 1600 (same confidence level and about the same p)?

The sample size is multiplied by 4, so the margin of error is multiplied by 1/√4 = 1/2: ME ≈ 0.025.

Explore (click to check your idea)

1. With p = 0.40 and n = 150, draw 1000 samples. What do the mean and SD of the p values show?

The mean is very close to 0.40 (p is an unbiased estimator of p) and the SD is close to √(0.40 · 0.60/150) = 0.04. About 95% of the values fall within 2 SD of p, between 0.32 and 0.48.

2. Change n from 150 to 600 (same p) and draw 1000 samples. What happens to the spread?

Quadrupling n cuts the standard deviation in half (0.04 → 0.02). The center stays at p: a larger sample reduces variability, not bias.

3. Set p = 0.05 and n = 30. Is the distribution Normal? Why?

No: it is skewed right and discrete. np = 1.5 is less than 10, so the large counts condition fails and the Normal model (orange curve) does not fit well.

4. Keep n = 150 and move p closer to 0.5. What changes in σp?

The spread is largest at p = 0.5 because p(1 − p) is largest there (0.25). For p = 0.9 and n = 150, σ = √(0.09/150) ≈ 0.0245 is smaller.
