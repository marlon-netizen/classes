Sampling Distribution for Sample Proportions

Unit 3 asks one big question: what can a sample proportion tell us about the population proportion? Lessons 3.1–3.4 take us from the idea of a sampling distribution, to the sampling distribution of p, to confidence intervals and the claims we can justify with them.

Objectives

  • Understand the concept of a sampling distribution.
  • Describe the center, spread and shape of the sampling distribution of a sample proportion.
  • Verify that the conditions are met for the Normal approximation of the sampling distribution of p.
  • Construct and interpret a confidence interval for a population proportion, and justify claims with it.
  • Preview: describe and check the conditions for the sampling distribution of a difference of sample proportions (see the last card).
p = number of successesn The sample proportion p (a statistic) estimates the population proportion p (a parameter).

Lesson 3.1 — Sampling distributions and estimators

LESSON 3.1

What is a sampling distribution?

  • A sample statistic (such as p) is a point estimator of a population parameter (such as p).
  • Estimators have variability: different random samples give different values. We model that variability with probability.
  • The sampling distribution of a statistic is the distribution of its values over all possible samples of the same size from the population.

Six key ideas (stated for sample means, and the same pattern appears for proportions):

  • Averages vary less than individual values.
  • Averages based on larger samples vary less than averages based on smaller samples.
  • Central Limit Theorem (CLT): when the sample size is sufficiently large, the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately Normal.
  • The larger the sample size n, the closer the sampling distribution of x is to a Normal distribution.
  • If the parent population is Normal, the sampling distribution of x is Normal for any sample size.
  • The larger the sample size, the closer the sample distribution is to the population distribution.
CLT implications: the CLT is about the shape of the sampling distribution of x, not its center or spread. Separately, μx = μ and σx ≈ σ/√n. For proportions, see Lesson 3.2.

Use the Sampling Sim tab to see a sampling distribution of p being built, one sample at a time.

LESSON 3.1

Biased and unbiased estimators

  • In a sampling distribution, bias means the distribution is not centered on the population parameter.
  • An estimator is unbiased if, on average, its value equals the parameter: the mean of its sampling distribution equals the parameter.
  • Unbiased: the sample proportion p (for p), the sample mean x (for μ) and the sample slope b (for β).
  • Biased: the sample maximum. The population maximum lies far to the right of the center of the sampling distribution of the sample maxima.
Good estimator = unbiased + low variability Among unbiased estimators, the one whose sampling distribution has less variability is the better estimator.
Example 1 — Choosing an estimator (quality control)

Five new estimators are evaluated for the weight of professional baseballs. Each is tested every day for a month on samples of size n = 10, 20 and 40. The baseballs produced that month had a consistent mean weight of 146 grams. The histograms of each estimator’s values (from the class presentation) show:

Lesson 3.2 — Sampling distribution of a sample proportion

LESSON 3.2

From counts to proportions

A proportion is about the presence or absence of an attribute: the population splits into “haves” and “have-nots”, and we count the “yes” answers. That is exactly a binomial setting, and when n is large enough the binomial is approximately Normal.

For the number of successes in a sample of size n:

mean = np   and   SD = √np(1 − p)

To change the number of successes into the proportion of successes we divide by n. Dividing every value by a constant divides both the mean and the standard deviation by that constant (Unit 1):

μp = p      σp = √p(1 − p) / n The sample proportion is an unbiased estimator of p, and the larger n, the tighter the clustering around p.

Conditions for using the Normal model for p:

  • Random: ideally a simple random sample (SRS).
  • Normality (large counts): np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10.
  • Independence (10% rule): n ≤ 0.10N. We are really worried about independence; randomly selecting fewer than 10% of the population lets us assume it. If the sample is large relative to the population, different inference techniques are needed.

When the conditions hold, p is approximately Normal, so probabilities come from z-scores or from a calculator (normalcdf(lower, upper, mean, sd)):

z = p − pσp
Example 2 — Which p allows a Normal model?

If we pick a simple random sample of size 80 from a large population, which of the following values of the population proportion p would allow use of the Normal model for the sampling distribution of p? (A) 0.10   (B) 0.15   (C) 0.90   (D) 0.95   (E) 0.99

Check np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10 with n = 80:

(A) 80(0.10) = 8 < 10 ✗   (B) 80(0.15) = 12 and 80(0.85) = 68 ✓   (C) 80(0.10) = 8 < 10 ✗   (D), (E) fewer than 10 failures ✗

The answer is (B) 0.15.

Example 3 — The 10% rule

A student wants the mean lip ratio (outer edges of lips / upper ridges of lips) for students in her high school, and plans to randomly sample 50 of the 780 students. Does this satisfy the 10% rule, and what is the purpose of the rule?

10% of 780 is 78, and 50 ≤ 78, so yes, the 10% rule is satisfied.

Purpose: when sampling without replacement, the observations are slightly dependent. Taking less than 10% of the population lets us treat the observations as independent, which the standard deviation formulas require.

Example 4 — A probability for p

It is estimated that 80% of people with high math anxiety experience brain activity similar to physical pain when anticipating a math problem. In a simple random sample of 110 people with high math anxiety, what is the probability that less than 75% experience this brain activity?

Conditions: the sample is random; np = 110(0.80) = 88 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) = 110(0.20) = 22 ≥ 10; and 110 is clearly less than 10% of all people with math anxiety.

So p is approximately Normal with mean 0.80 and σp = √(0.80 · 0.20/110) = 0.0381.

normalcdf(−100, 0.75, 0.80, 0.0381) = 0.0947 (equivalently, z = (0.75 − 0.80)/0.0381 ≈ −1.31).

In conclusion: getting a sample proportion of 0.75 or less happens in about 9.5% of all possible samples of size 110 from this population.

Lesson 3.3 — Confidence interval for a population proportion

LESSON 3.3

Confidence intervals: the idea

Using what we know about sampling distributions, we can say how confident we are that a sample proportion lies within a margin of error of p, and then have the same confidence that p lies within that margin of the sample proportion. A confidence interval has two parts:

  • The interval of plausible values: estimate ± margin of error.
  • The confidence level: the success rate of the method, i.e. the proportion of times repeated applications of the method would capture the true parameter. We do not say there is a 0.90, 0.95 or 0.99 probability that p is within the margin of error of one given sample proportion.

Conditions for inference: independence (random sampling and the 10% rule) and Normality of the sampling distribution. For a proportion, Normality means np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10. (For means you will check that the parent population is approximately Normal, or that n ≥ 30, or that small samples are free of strong skewness and outliers.)

LESSON 3.3

Standard error, margin of error and the interval

The formula for σp needs the true p, which we do not know. The reasonable fix is to use p instead; the estimated standard deviation is called the standard error:

σp ≈ SEp = √p(1 − p) / n

Standard error vs margin of error: the standard error measures how far the sample statistic typically varies from the parameter. The margin of error is a multiple of the standard error, ME = z* · SE, where the multiple is set by how confident we want to be.

p ± z* · SEp point estimate ± margin of error

z* depends on the confidence level. On a graphing calculator find it with invNorm using the central area (for 95%, the lower tail is 0.025 and z* = 1.96). Common values:

Common critical values z*
Confidence level80%90%95%98%99%

The calculator command 1-PropZInt(x, n, C-level) returns the interval directly.

LESSON 3.3

How to write the solution

  1. Parameter: define p in context (“p = the proportion of [population] who [success]”).
  2. Procedure: name it — one-sample z-interval for a population proportion.
  3. Checks: independence (random sample; n ≤ 10% of the population) and Normality (np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10).
  4. Mechanics: compute p, SE, ME and the interval (or use 1-PropZInt).
  5. Conclusion in context: “We are C% confident that the interval from a to b captures the true proportion of [population] who [success].”
Example 5 — A 99% interval

(a) If 42% of a simple random sample of 550 young adults say that whoever asks for the date should pay for the first date, find a 99% confidence interval for the true proportion of all young adults who would say so. (b) Does the interval give convincing evidence that fewer than 50% would say so? (c) Interpret the confidence level.

(a) p = the proportion of all young adults who would say that whoever asks for the date should pay. Procedure: one-sample z-interval for p.

Checks: the sample is an SRS and 550 is clearly less than 10% of all young adults; np = 550(0.42) = 231 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) = 550(0.58) = 319 ≥ 10.

Mechanics: z* = 2.576; SE = √(0.42 · 0.58/550) ≈ 0.021; ME = 2.576(0.021) ≈ 0.054; interval = 0.42 ± 0.054 = (0.366, 0.474).

Conclusion: we are 99% confident that the interval from 0.366 to 0.474 captures the true proportion of young adults who would say that whoever asks should pay for the first date.

(b) Yes. The interval gives plausible values, and all of them are less than 0.50, so there is convincing evidence that fewer than 50% would say so.

(c) If all possible samples of 550 young adults were interviewed, about 99% of the resulting confidence intervals would capture the true proportion.

Example 6 — A 95% interval and a claim

(a) In a simple random sample of couples kissing in public places (airports, beaches, parks), 80 out of 124 leaned their heads to the right. Establish a 95% confidence interval for the true proportion of public kissing couples who lean their heads to the right. (b) A sociologist claims that over 60% of all public kissing couples lean right. Does the interval give convincing evidence for the claim? (c) Interpret the confidence level.

(a) Parameter: p = the proportion of all public kissing couples who lean their heads to the right. Procedure: one-sample z-interval for p.

Checks: SRS; we assume 124 is less than 10% of all public kissing couples; np = 80 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) = 44 ≥ 10.

Mechanics: 1-PropZInt(80, 124, 0.95) = (0.561, 0.729).

Conclusion: we are 95% confident that the interval from 0.561 to 0.729 captures the true proportion of all public kissing couples who lean their heads to the right.

(b) No. The interval (0.561, 0.729) contains values below 0.60 as well as above it, so it does not give convincing evidence that over 60% lean right.

(c) If all possible samples of 124 kissing couples were observed, about 95% of the resulting intervals would capture the true proportion.

Example 7 — Finding the confidence level from the margin of error

In a telephone survey of a random sample of 1000 high school seniors, 832 answered yes to “Do you want to get married someday?” (a) What is the parameter? (b) Are the conditions satisfied? (c) With what confidence can it be asserted that 83.2% ± 3% of the high school population want to get married someday?

(a) p = the proportion of all high school seniors who would answer yes to the question.

(b) Random sample, and 1000 is clearly less than 10% of all high school seniors; np = 832 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) = 168 ≥ 10. Yes.

(c) p = 832/1000 = 0.832, so SE = √(0.832 · 0.168/1000) = 0.0118. We need z*(0.0118) = 0.03, so z* = 0.03/0.0118 ≈ 2.54. Then normalcdf(−2.54, 2.54) = 0.9889.

So 83.2% ± 3% is a 98.89% confidence interval for the proportion of all high school seniors who would answer yes.

LESSON 3.3

Sample size for a desired margin of error

Solve ME = z*√(p(1 − p)/n) for n:

n ≥ (z*ME)2 · p(1 − p) If there is no prior estimate, use p = 0.5 (the most conservative value). Always round n up.
Example 8 — How many people?

A pollster wants a 95% interval with a margin of error of at most 0.03 and has no prior estimate of p.

n ≥ (1.96/0.03)²(0.5)(0.5) = 4268.4 × 0.25 ≈ 1067.1, so n = 1068.

Lesson 3.4 — Interpreting intervals and justifying claims

LESSON 3.4

Two kinds of statements

Two different statements come out of a confidence interval. Suppose (0.60, 0.66) is a 90% confidence interval for the proportion of all voters who favor a bond issue.

  • Interpret the interval: “We are 90% confident that the interval from 0.60 to 0.66 captures the true proportion of all voters who favor the bond issue.” (Between 60% and 66% of all voters favor it.)
  • Interpret the confidence level: “If this survey were conducted many times, about 90% of the resulting confidence intervals would contain the true proportion of voters who favor the bond issue.”

Three common misconceptions about the 90% interval (0.60, 0.66):

MisconceptionFact
#1: “The true proportion of all voters is between 0.60 and 0.66.”The true proportion may or may not be in the interval; we are only 90% confident that it is.
#2: “There is a 0.90 probability that the true proportion is between 0.60 and 0.66.”For a given interval, the true proportion either is or is not inside it, so the probability is 1 or 0. The 90% describes the method.
#3: “If the survey were repeated, about 90% of the sample proportions would be in (0.60, 0.66).”(0.60, 0.66) is one specific interval from one sample proportion; it says nothing about other samples’ proportions. The 90% refers to the intervals capturing p.
LESSON 3.4

Justifying a claim from an interval

  • The interval is a set of plausible values for p.
  • If a claimed value is inside the interval, it is plausible: no convincing evidence against it.
  • If the claimed value is outside the interval, the interval gives convincing evidence that the claim is not true.
  • “Fewer than 50%” is supported only if all plausible values are below 0.50; “over 60%” is supported only if all are above 0.60.
  • Why can an interval miss p? Random sampling variability: a 95% interval fails to capture p in about 5% of samples. Watch it in the CI Sim tab.

Example 9 — Using an interval to evaluate claims

A 95% confidence interval for the proportion of teens who sleep fewer than 8 hours on school nights is (0.58, 0.66). (a) Is there convincing evidence that a majority of teens sleep fewer than 8 hours? (b) A website claims the proportion is 0.70. What does the interval say?

(a) All plausible values (0.58 to 0.66) are above 0.50, so there is convincing evidence that more than half of teens sleep fewer than 8 hours.

(b) 0.70 is not in the interval, so the interval gives convincing evidence that the true proportion is not 0.70.

Example 10 — Margin of error and sample size

A survey with n = 400 has a margin of error of 0.05 at 95% confidence. What margin of error would you expect with n = 1600 (same confidence level and about the same p)?

The sample size is multiplied by 4, so the margin of error is multiplied by 1/√4 = 1/2: ME ≈ 0.025.

LOOKING AHEAD

Difference of two sample proportions

The unit objectives also mention the sampling distribution of a difference of sample proportions, p1 − p2, from two independent random samples. The class presentation lists it as an objective without worked content, so here is only the preview you will need later:

  • Center: μ = p1 − p2.
  • Spread (independent samples): σ = √( p1(1 − p1)/n1 + p2(1 − p2)/n2 ).
  • Conditions: both samples random and independent of each other; each sample is under 10% of its population; n1p1, n1(1 − p1), n2p2 and n2(1 − p2) are all at least 10.

1. With p = 0.40 and n = 150, draw 1000 samples. What do the mean and SD of the p values show?

The mean is very close to 0.40 (p is an unbiased estimator of p) and the SD is close to √(0.40 · 0.60/150) = 0.04. About 95% of the values fall within 2 SD of p, between 0.32 and 0.48.

2. Change n from 150 to 600 (same p) and draw 1000 samples. What happens to the spread?

Quadrupling n cuts the standard deviation in half (0.04 → 0.02). The center stays at p: a larger sample reduces variability, not bias.

3. Set p = 0.05 and n = 30. Is the distribution Normal? Why?

No: it is skewed right and discrete. np = 1.5 is less than 10, so the large counts condition fails and the Normal model (orange curve) does not fit well.

4. Keep n = 150 and move p closer to 0.5. What changes in σp?

The spread is largest at p = 0.5 because p(1 − p) is largest there (0.25). For p = 0.9 and n = 150, σ = √(0.09/150) ≈ 0.0245 is smaller.

1. Use 95% with p = 0.50 and n = 100. Draw 100 intervals several times. How many capture p?

About 95 out of 100 (it varies from batch to batch, such as 92 or 97), and about 5 miss. The 95% describes the long-run success rate of the method, not the probability that one particular interval contains p: each interval either captures it or does not.

2. Switch to 99%. What happens to the number of misses and to the width of the intervals?

Misses drop to about 1 in 100, but the intervals become wider because z* grows from 1.960 to 2.576. More confidence costs precision.

3. Go back to 95% and increase n from 100 to 400. What changes?

The intervals are about half as wide (the margin of error is divided by √4 = 2), but the capture rate is still about 95%. A bigger sample gives precision; it does not change the confidence level.

4. Set p = 0.05, n = 30 at 95% and draw 1000 intervals. Is the capture rate still near 95%?

No, it is noticeably lower (around 80%). With np often below 10, the large counts condition fails and the z-interval does not work as advertised. Conditions matter!
