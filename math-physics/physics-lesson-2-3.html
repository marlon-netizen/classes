Part A

Free fall

On August 2, 1971, astronaut David Scott released a hammer and a feather from the same height above the Moon's surface. Although the hammer is far more massive, both fell at the same rate: they traveled the same displacement in the same amount of time.

In a vacuum (Figure 3.1) an apple and a feather released from rest stay side by side. The time between successive images is the same, but the displacement in each interval grows by the same amount each time — so the velocity increases by the same amount every interval. They fall with the same constant acceleration.

Key term Free fall — the motion of a body when only the force due to gravity is acting on the body.

If air resistance is ignored, every object dropped near the surface of a planet falls with the same acceleration, directed toward the center of the planet. We write it ag (or g). Choosing up as the positive direction:

ag = −g = −9.81 m/s² (≈ −32 ft/s²)

Unless stated otherwise, use 9.81 m/s². We use Δy (vertical displacement) instead of Δx for vertical motion.

Part B

Acceleration is constant during upward and downward motion

A ball thrown straight up keeps moving upward for a while, stops momentarily at the peak, then falls. Because the ball changes direction it may seem that velocity and acceleration are both changing. In fact, the acceleration is −9.81 m/s² from the instant of release until it lands.

Example (Figure 3.2): a ball is thrown up with vi = +10.5 m/s. After 1.0 s its velocity has changed by −9.81 m/s, to +0.69 m/s. After 2.0 s it changes by another −9.81 m/s, to −9.12 m/s.

Big ideaAt the peak the velocity is zero, but the acceleration is still −9.81 m/s². The straight-line slope of the velocity–time graph (Figure 3.3) shows the acceleration is constant at every moment.
  • Rising: velocity +, acceleration − → slowing down (smaller and smaller displacements per interval).
  • At the top: velocity 0, acceleration −9.81 m/s² (momentarily at rest).
  • Falling: velocity −, acceleration − → speeding up (larger and larger displacements per interval).

Why it matters

Skydiving and terminal velocity

If Earth had no atmosphere, a skydiver would fall with ag = −9.81 m/s² forever. In reality air resistance grows with speed: the acceleration decreases and, after a few seconds, drops to zero. The constant speed reached is the terminal velocity; it depends on the object's mass, shape and size.

Body position

The world record for the fastest terminal velocity (2015) was 148 m/s (331 mi/h).

Take it further

Angular kinematics

A point on an object that rotates about a fixed axis moves in a circle. We describe it with angular quantities: all points on a rigid rotating object (except on the axis) turn through the same angle in the same time.

Measuring angles with radians

θ = arc lengthradius = sr

The radian (rad) is a pure number: one radian is the angle for which the arc length equals the radius. One full revolution: s = 2πr → θ = 2π rad ≈ 6.28 rad. To convert degrees to radians:

θ (rad) = π180° θ (deg)
Key termsAngular displacement Δθ = Δs/r · Angular velocity ωavg = Δθ/Δt (rad/s) · Angular acceleration αavg = Δω/Δt = (ω2 − ω1)/(t2 − t1) (rad/s²). Counterclockwise is positive; clockwise is negative. α has the same sign as ω when the rotation speeds up.

Physics on the edge

Special relativity and time dilation

Before Einstein, everyone assumed Δt was the same for every observer (time is "absolute"). In 1905 he showed that the speed of light is the same for any observer, so the observer beside the track, who sees the light pulse travel a longer path than the passenger on the train, must measure a longer time interval. This is time dilation:

Δt′ = Δt√(1 − v²/c²)

Δt is measured by the person on the train (or the moving clock); Δt′ by the person beside the track; c = 3.00 × 108 m/s. At everyday speeds v²/c² is tiny, so Δt′ ≈ Δt. Near the speed of light the effect grows without limit. It has been confirmed with muons traveling at 0.9994c and with atomic clocks on jet planes.

Think Science

Theories in motion

A hypothesis is a tentative, testable explanation supported by evidence. When a hypothesis proves reliable across a wide range of conditions, scientists combine it with related hypotheses to form a theory.

Falling objects. Aristotle (4th century BCE) claimed that the speed of a falling object is directly related to its mass; this qualitative idea was accepted for nearly 2000 years. By about 1600 Galileo theorized that objects fall at the same speed, independent of mass, and supported it with experimental and observational evidence. Independent tests and Newton's theory of gravitation later displaced Aristotle's explanation.

Remember when solving problems

  1. Analyze — list givens and unknowns; choose up as +; remember a = −9.81 m/s² and, for objects dropped, vi = 0.
  2. Plan — choose the equation that has your unknown and leaves out the quantity you don't know and don't need.
  3. Solve — rearrange first, substitute with signs, and choose the sign of any square root by reasoning about direction.
  4. Check — does the sign make sense (down = negative)? Is the size reasonable compared with the free-fall acceleration?
