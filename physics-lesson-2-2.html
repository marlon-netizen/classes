Part A

Changes in velocity

A bullet train has a top speed of about 300 km/h, but it stops to load passengers, so most of the time its velocity is either increasing or decreasing. When a shuttle bus slows from 9.0 m/s to 0 m/s the change in velocity is the same whether the driver brakes gently over 5.0 s or slams the brakes in 1.5 s — what differs is the time interval over which the change happens. That difference changes the comfort and safety of the passengers.

Key term Acceleration — the rate at which velocity changes over time. An object accelerates if its speed, its direction, or both change.

Part B

Direction and sign of acceleration

Acceleration has direction and magnitude. Choose a positive direction (for example, to the right). Then:

  • If velocity is constant, Δv = 0 and a = 0.
  • If Δv is positive, a is positive; if Δv is negative, a is negative.
  • A negative acceleration does not always mean "slowing down." For an object moving in the negative direction, a negative acceleration means it is speeding up.
  • "Deceleration" is just the special case where the speed decreases.
Rule of thumbSame signs for velocity and acceleration → speeding up. Opposite signs → slowing down.

Velocity–time graphs

Part D

Kinematic equations for constant acceleration

These equations are valid only when acceleration is constant (the problem often says "uniform acceleration"). They relate five quantities: vi, vf, a, Δx, and Δt. Each equation leaves out exactly one of the five.

EquationLeaves outUse it when you don't know / need…
Δx = ½(vi + vf)Δtaacceleration
vf = vi + aΔtΔxdisplacement
Δx = viΔt + ½a(Δt)2vffinal velocity
vf2 = vi2 + 2aΔxΔttime

If the object starts from rest, vi = 0 and the equations simplify: Δx = ½vfΔt, vf = aΔt, Δx = ½a(Δt)², vf2 = 2aΔx.

Where do the equations come from?
  1. Average velocity for constant acceleration is vavg = (vi + vf)/2, and vavg = Δx/Δt, so Δx = ½(vi + vf)Δt.
  2. Rearranging a = (vf − vi)/Δt gives vf = vi + aΔt.
  3. Substituting that vf into the first equation gives Δx = viΔt + ½a(Δt)².
  4. Solving the first equation for Δt and substituting into vf = vi + aΔt gives vf² = vi² + 2aΔx. Taking the square root gives ±; choose the sign by thinking about the direction of motion.

Remember when solving problems

  1. Analyze — list the givens and the unknown, convert units (km/h → m/s: ÷ 3.6; min → s: × 60) and choose a positive direction.
  2. Plan — choose the equation that has your unknown and leaves out the quantity you have no information about.
  3. Solve — rearrange first, then substitute. Keep the signs of vectors (velocity, acceleration, displacement).
  4. Check — do the sign and the size make sense? Does the object speed up or slow down as expected?
