What you will learn

Between 1750 and 1912, ideas about freedom, equality and justice crossed oceans and shaped the new nations of Latin America, including Nicaragua. This unit follows those ideas and the people who turned them into action.

Core concepts

Enlightenment, French Revolution and the legacy of Simón Bolívar

The Enlightenment was an eighteenth-century European movement that trusted reason more than tradition or absolute monarchy. Its thinkers argued that people have rights by nature and that government exists to protect them.

John Locke

Natural rights: life, liberty and property. A government that violates them loses legitimacy.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Popular sovereignty: power belongs to the people, who form a "social contract."

Montesquieu

Separation of powers: legislative, executive and judicial branches check each other.

Voltaire

Tolerance and free expression against religious fanaticism and censorship.

The French Revolution (1789)

The French people overthrew the absolute monarchy and the privileges of nobles and clergy. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen proclaimed liberty, equality and national sovereignty.

"Men are born and remain free and equal in rights."Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, 1789, Article 1

How the ideas reached Latin America

  • Educated criollos (American-born people of Spanish descent) read Enlightenment works and resented colonial rules that gave top posts to Spain-born officials.
  • Antonio Nariño translated and printed the Declaration of Rights in Bogotá in 1794.
  • Other models: the independence of the United States (1776) and the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), the first successful uprising of enslaved people.
  • In 1808 Napoleon invaded Spain. The crown's authority collapsed, and local juntas began claiming that sovereignty returned to the people.

Simón Bolívar (1783–1830), "El Libertador"

Born in Caracas, Bolívar studied under Simón Rodríguez and read Enlightenment authors. After a trip to Europe he promised to free his homeland. He led armies that liberated what are now Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia.

  • Jamaica Letter (1815): analyzes why Spanish America deserved independence and imagines possible futures for the new nations.
  • Angostura Address (1819): argues for a strong republic with separation of powers; the Republic of Gran Colombia is founded the same year.
  • Congress of Panama (1826): his proposal for an alliance of the new republics, the dream of the Patria Grande, a united Latin America.
  • He died in 1830, disappointed by the divisions among the new countries, yet his legacy of freedom and unity remains.
Nicaraguan connection: Central America declared independence from Spain on 15 September 1821. Bolívar's call for American unity and the ideas of sovereignty later inspired Nicaraguan thinkers such as Rubén Darío and Benjamín Zeledón.

Marxist philosophy: context, characteristics and transformations

Karl Marx (1818–1883) and Friedrich Engels (1820–1895) developed their ideas in nineteenth-century Europe, where the Industrial Revolution created huge wealth alongside harsh factory work, child labor, low wages and crowded cities.

Influences

  • German philosophy (Hegel): history moves through contradictions, called the dialectic. Marx gave this a material basis.
  • French utopian socialism (Saint-Simon, Fourier): imagined fairer societies, but without a method to build them.
  • British political economy (Adam Smith, David Ricardo): analysis of labor, value and capital.

Main characteristics

Historical materialism

How people produce what they need (the economic base) shapes laws, politics, religion and ideas (the superstructure).

Class struggle

Societies are divided between those who own the means of production (bourgeoisie) and those who sell their labor (proletariat).

Surplus value & alienation

Owners keep value that workers create beyond their wages; workers become estranged from their product and themselves.

Social transformation

Capitalism would be replaced by socialism and, eventually, a classless society without exploitation.
"The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."Marx and Engels, The Communist Manifesto, 1848

Key works and impact

  • The Communist Manifesto (1848) and Das Kapital (1867).
  • Inspired labor unions, socialist parties and revolutions, including Russia in 1917.
  • In Latin America, thinkers like José Carlos Mariátegui (Peru) adapted Marxism to Indigenous and peasant realities, and it influenced twentieth-century liberation movements.
  • It is also debated and criticized: scholars discuss its predictions, its economic theory and the political systems built in its name.
Nicaraguan connection: Questions about inequality, land and who benefits from a country's wealth appear throughout Nicaraguan history. Studying Marxism helps you analyze those debates critically.

Latin American philosophical thought and Rubén Darío

Latin American thought asks: Who are we? How can we be free and just on our own terms? Thinkers combined European ideas with local realities, Indigenous heritage and the experience of colonialism.

Simón Rodríguez

Educator who argued that Americans must create their own original institutions.

Andrés Bello

Venezuelan scholar; shaped education, grammar and law in the new republics.

José Martí (Cuba)

Nuestra América (1891): know our own history and resist outside domination.

José Enrique Rodó (Uruguay)

Ariel (1900): defended spiritual and cultural values against pure materialism.

José Carlos Mariátegui (Peru)

Linked Marxism with the situation of Indigenous peoples.

Paulo Freire & Enrique Dussel

Pedagogy of the Oppressed and the philosophy of liberation, twentieth century.

Rubén Darío (1867–1916)

Born in Metapa (today Ciudad Darío), Nicaragua, he became the leading voice of Modernismo, the first Latin American literary movement to influence Spain and the world. He renewed the Spanish language with new rhythms, images and cosmopolitan themes.

  • Azul… (1888): launched Modernismo. Prosas profanas (1896) and Cantos de vida y esperanza (1905) followed.
  • His poem "A Roosevelt" warns about United States expansion in Latin America and defends Hispanic identity.
  • He promoted cultural unity among Spanish-speaking nations, a cultural form of the Patria Grande.
  • He died in León, Nicaragua, in 1916; his tomb is in the León Cathedral. He is called the "Prince of Castilian letters."
"Eres los Estados Unidos, eres el futuro invasor de la América ingenua que tiene sangre indígena, que aún reza a Jesucristo y aún habla en español."Rubén Darío, "A Roosevelt," 1904
Nicaraguan connection: Darío made Nicaragua a literary capital of the Spanish-speaking world. Each year the Rubén Darío International Poetry Festival is held in Granada, and León hosts his museum and tomb.

Benjamín Zeledón: anti-imperialism, ethics, patriotism and sovereignty

Benjamín Zeledón Rodríguez (1879–1912) was a lawyer, Liberal soldier and patriot, now honored as a National Hero of Nicaragua.

Historical context

  • President José Santos Zelaya (Liberal Revolution of 1893) pursued modernization and an independent course. His government came into conflict with the United States over canal and financial interests.
  • In 1909 a Conservative rebellion, backed by U.S. interests, and the U.S. Knox Note pushed Zelaya out. The United States then supported conservative governments, including that of Adolfo Díaz.
  • In 1912, when Díaz asked for U.S. military help, U.S. Marines landed in Nicaragua.

His thought and actions

Influences

Liberal and Enlightenment ideas of law and rights, Bolívar's call for American unity, and Latin American nationalism.

Letters & proclamations

He wrote to foreign officials and to the Nicaraguan people, rejecting foreign intervention and calling on citizens to defend the homeland.

Anti-imperialism

He opposed governments kept in power by foreign troops and the loss of national decision-making.

Ethics & patriotism

He refused personal advantage, accepted sacrifice and put the nation before himself.

The 1912 campaign and death

  • He led the patriotic resistance against the Díaz government and U.S. intervention, and gathered popular support.
  • On 3–4 October 1912 he defended the hills of Coyotepe and La Barranca near Masaya. Superior U.S. and government forces prevailed.
  • He was captured and executed on 4 October 1912 in Niquinohomo. He is buried in Catarina.
  • In Niquinohomo, a young Augusto C. Sandino witnessed these events. The memory later inspired Sandino's own struggle for sovereignty.
Why he matters: Zeledón links the Enlightenment idea of popular sovereignty and Bolívar's dream of free American nations to a concrete Nicaraguan defense of national independence.
Nicaraguan connection: This is part of national heritage. Historic sites such as Coyotepe Fortress and the town of Catarina keep his memory alive.
