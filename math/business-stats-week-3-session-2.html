Random variables and distributions

A random variable X assigns a number to each outcome of a random process (units demanded today, number of defective items, loss on a loan). It is discrete if it takes countable values (0, 1, 2, …) and continuous if it can take any value in an interval (Week 4).

A probability distribution lists every value x with its probability P(x):

0 ≤ P(x) ≤ 1 for every x Σ P(x) = 1
Check first

Before using any table, confirm that all probabilities are between 0 and 1 and that they add to exactly 1.

Expected value, variance and standard deviation

μ = E(X) = Σ x · P(x)Expected value: the probability-weighted average, i.e. the long-run mean
σ² = Var(X) = Σ (x − μ)² · P(x) = E(X²) − μ² σ = √σ²Variance and standard deviation: the typical spread around the mean

Worked example · daily demand

The distribution of daily demand is shown below. Each unit sold earns $40 and fixed costs are $50 per day. Find E(X), SD(X), and the mean and standard deviation of daily profit.

  1. E(X) = Σ x·P(x) = 0(0.05) + 1(0.20) + 2(0.35) + 3(0.25) + 4(0.15) = 2.25 units.
  2. Var(X) = Σ (x − 2.25)² P(x) = 1.1875; SD(X) = √1.1875 = 1.090 units.
  3. Profit = 40X − 50. E(profit) = 40(2.25) − 50 = $40.00.
  4. SD(profit) = 40 × SD(X) = $43.59 (the −50 shifts the mean only).
Answer: Mean demand 2.25 units (SD 1.090); mean profit $40 per day with SD $43.59.

Expected value is not “the likely outcome”

It is the average over many repetitions. A fair business comparison needs both the expected value (reward) and the standard deviation (risk): two projects with the same mean can have very different risk.

Excel

With x in A2:A6 and P in B2:B6: =SUMPRODUCT(A2:A6,B2:B6) for the mean, =SUMPRODUCT((A2:A6-mean)^2,B2:B6) for the variance. Use =BINOM.DIST(x,n,p,FALSE) and =POISSON.DIST(x,λ,FALSE) for named distributions (TRUE gives the cumulative P(X ≤ x)).

Standard deviations do not add

For independent X and Y: SD(X + Y) = √(σX² + σY²), not σX + σY. (Independent risks partly offset each other: the basis of diversification.)

Binomial distribution

Use it when a process has n independent trials, each with the same success probability p, and X counts successes (defects in a sample of 20, customers who buy out of 10).

P(X = x) = C(n, x) · px · (1 − p)n − x μ = np σ = √(np(1 − p))where C(n, x) = n! / (x!(n − x)!)

Worked example · binomial probabilities

Each of 10 customers buys independently with probability 0.20. Find P(at least one buys) and the mean and SD of the number of buyers.

  1. P(none) = C(10,0)·0.2⁰·0.8¹⁰ = 0.8¹⁰ = 0.1074.
  2. P(at least one) = 1 − 0.1074 = 0.8926.
  3. Mean = np = 10 × 0.20 = 2; SD = √(10 × 0.2 × 0.8) = √1.6 = 1.265.
Answer: P(≥ 1) = 0.8926; mean 2 buyers, SD 1.265.

Poisson distribution (brief)

For counts of events in a fixed interval of time or space with average rate λ (calls per minute, defects per metre): P(x) = e−λλx/x!, with mean = variance = λ. Example: λ = 3 calls/minute → P(2 calls) = 0.2240.

Common mistakes

  • Using a table whose probabilities do not add to 1.
  • Reporting E(X) as the value that will occur.
  • Forgetting to square the multiplier in the variance rule, or adding standard deviations.
  • Using the binomial when trials are not independent or p changes.
  • Computing P(X ≥ 1) by adding many terms instead of 1 − P(0).
  • Mixing up Var(X) and SD(X) in the final sentence.
