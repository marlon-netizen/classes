Comparing Two Population Proportions

So far, Unit 3 has dealt with one population proportion. Many real questions compare two groups: Is the proportion of patients who recover higher with Drug A than with Drug B? Do two cities differ in support for a proposal? Lessons 3.9–3.11 extend what you know to the difference between two population proportions, p1 − p2: first its sampling distribution (3.9), then a confidence interval (3.10), and then using the interval to justify claims (3.11).

Objectives

  • Describe the center, spread and shape of the sampling distribution of the difference of two sample proportions, and verify its conditions.
  • Identify the procedure and the parameter, verify conditions, and construct a confidence interval for the difference of two population proportions (standard error and margin of error).
  • Interpret the interval in context and justify a claim about the difference, including what it means when 0 is or is not in the interval.

Lesson 3.9 — Sampling distribution of a difference in sample proportions

LESSON 3.9

From one proportion to two

We have two populations (or two treatments) with population proportions p1 and p2, and we take an independent random sample of size n1 from the first and n2 from the second, getting sample proportions p1 and p2. The statistic of interest is the difference p1 − p2, and its sampling distribution describes how that difference varies from pair of samples to pair of samples.

FeatureSampling distribution of p1 − p2
Centerμ = p1 − p2: the difference of sample proportions is an unbiased estimator of the difference of population proportions.
Spreadσ = √( p1(1 − p1)/n1 + p2(1 − p2)/n2 ), valid when the samples are independent.
Shapeapproximately Normal when the large counts conditions hold in both groups.
Why variances add: for independent random variables, Var(X − Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y). The difference of two noisy statistics is noisier than either one, so σ² = σ1² + σ2² and σ = √(σ1² + σ2²). Standard deviations themselves do not add.
LESSON 3.9

Conditions

  • Independence within each group: each sample is a random sample (or, in an experiment, subjects are randomly assigned), and when sampling without replacement each sample is at most 10% of its population: n1 ≤ 0.10N1 and n2 ≤ 0.10N2.
  • Independence between groups: the two samples (or groups) are independent of each other. Paired or repeated measurements on the same individuals are not independent, so these methods do not apply.
  • Normality (large counts), in each group: n1p1 ≥ 10, n1(1 − p1) ≥ 10, n2p2 ≥ 10 and n2(1 − p2) ≥ 10.

When the conditions hold, probabilities come from a Normal model, with a z-score of the form

z = (p1 − p2) − (p1 − p2)σp1 − p2

Example 1 — Mean, standard deviation and a probability

The proportion of customers satisfied with service is 0.60 for Store 1 and 0.50 for Store 2. Independent random samples of n1 = 100 and n2 = 120 customers are taken (each far less than 10% of the customers). (a) Describe the sampling distribution of p1 − p2. (b) Find the probability that Store 1’s sample proportion is less than Store 2’s.

Conditions: independent random samples, under 10% of each population; 100(0.60) = 60, 100(0.40) = 40, 120(0.50) = 60 and 120(0.50) = 60 are all ≥ 10. So the distribution is approximately Normal.

(a) Mean = 0.60 − 0.50 = 0.10. σ = √(0.60 · 0.40/100 + 0.50 · 0.50/120) = √(0.0024 + 0.002083) = √0.004483 ≈ 0.0670.

(b) P(p1 − p2 < 0): z = (0 − 0.10)/0.0670 ≈ -1.493, so the probability is 0.0677. About 6.8% of such pairs of samples would show Store 1 lower, even though Store 1 is really better.

Example 2 — Which situation allows the methods?

For each study, decide whether the independence between groups condition holds: (a) 40 students are randomly assigned to study with Method A or Method B and their pass/fail results are compared; (b) 60 shoppers are asked “Do you like the new logo?” before and after seeing an ad, and the proportions of “yes” are compared.

(a) Random assignment to two separate groups: independent groups, so the two-sample methods apply (check the large counts too: with only 20 per group the counts may be too small).

(b) The same people answer twice, so the two proportions are dependent (paired): the two-sample procedures for proportions do not apply.

Lesson 3.10 — Confidence interval for a difference of two proportions

LESSON 3.10

The two-sample z-interval for p1 − p2

We do not know p1 and p2, so we use the sample proportions in the standard deviation. The estimated standard deviation is the standard error, and the interval is point estimate ± margin of error:

(p1 − p2) ± z* · SE SE = √( p1(1 − p1)/n1 + p2(1 − p2)/n2 )  |  ME = z* · SE
  • The calculator command is 2-PropZInt: enter x1, n1, x2, n2 and the confidence level.
  • Critical values z*: 90% → 1.645, 95% → 1.960, 99% → 2.576.
  • Use the sample proportions separately (no pooling) in an interval.
  • The order of subtraction matters: define which group is group 1. Reversing the order flips the signs of both limits.
LESSON 3.10

Conditions and write-up

  • Independence: two independent random samples (or randomized assignment of subjects to two treatments); each sample at most 10% of its population.
  • Normality: at least 10 successes and at least 10 failures in each sample: n1p1, n1(1 − p1), n2p2 and n2(1 − p2) all ≥ 10.
  1. Parameter: define p1 and p2 in context (which group is 1, which is 2) and the parameter p1 − p2.
  2. Procedure: two-sample z-interval for a difference of two population proportions.
  3. Checks: independence and the four large-counts checks.
  4. Mechanics: p1, p2, SE, ME and the interval (or 2-PropZInt).
  5. Conclusion in context: “We are C% confident that the interval from ___ to ___ captures the true difference in the proportion of [population 1] who [success] and the proportion of [population 2] who [success].”
Example 3 — A 95% interval from two random samples

A random sample of 200 adults who used a sleep app and an independent random sample of 220 adults who did not were asked whether they sleep better. In the app group 124 said yes; in the other group 110 said yes. Construct and interpret a 95% confidence interval for the difference in the proportions of all such adults who sleep better.

Parameter: let p1 = the proportion of all adults who use the app and report better sleep, and p2 = the proportion of all adults who do not use the app and report better sleep. We estimate p1 − p2.

Procedure: two-sample z-interval for p1 − p2.

Checks: independent random samples (we assume each is under 10% of its population); counts 124, 76, 110 and 110 are all ≥ 10.

Mechanics: p1 = 124/200 = 0.62, p2 = 110/220 = 0.50, difference = 0.12. SE = √(0.62(0.3800)/200 + 0.50(0.5000)/220) ≈ 0.0481. ME = 1.96(0.0481) ≈ 0.0943. Interval = 0.12 ± 0.0943 = (0.026, 0.214).

Conclusion: we are 95% confident that the interval from 0.026 to 0.214 captures the true difference in the proportion of all adults who use the app and sleep better and the proportion of all adults who do not use the app and sleep better.

Example 4 — A 90% interval from a randomized experiment

120 patients were randomly assigned to Drug A and 130 to Drug B. In group A 78 recovered; in group B 65 recovered. Construct a 90% confidence interval for the difference in the recovery proportions (A minus B).

Parameter: pA − pB, the difference in the true recovery proportions for patients like these given Drug A and Drug B.

Checks: subjects were randomly assigned to the two treatments; counts 78, 42, 65 and 65 are all ≥ 10.

Mechanics: pA = 0.65, pB = 0.50, difference = 0.15. SE ≈ 0.0618, ME = 1.645(0.0618) ≈ 0.1017. Interval = (0.048, 0.252).

Conclusion: we are 90% confident that the interval from 0.048 to 0.252 captures the true difference in the recovery proportion for patients given Drug A and for those given Drug B.

Lesson 3.11 — Justifying a claim from the interval

LESSON 3.11

Interpreting the interval and the confidence level

  • The interval: “We are C% confident that the interval from a to b captures the true difference in the proportion of [population 1] who [success] and the proportion of [population 2] who [success].” Always state the order of subtraction.
  • The confidence level: if we repeatedly took independent random samples of these sizes from the two populations and built an interval from each pair, about C% of the intervals would capture the true difference p1 − p2.
  • Still wrong: “There is a C% probability that the true difference is in this interval”; “C% of the sample differences are in this interval”.
LESSON 3.11

What the interval says about a claim

The key value to look for is 0, which means “no difference” (p1 = p2).

The interval for p1 − p2Conclusion
contains 0 (for example (−0.13, 0.07))0 is plausible: not convincing evidence of a difference between the population proportions. (We do not conclude they are equal.)
all values positive (for example (0.03, 0.21))Convincing evidence that p1 > p2: group 1 has the higher proportion.
all values negative (for example (−0.20, −0.04))Convincing evidence that p1 < p2.
  • The interval also gives the plausible size of the difference: an interval such as (0.002, 0.118) excludes 0 but allows a tiny difference, so judge practical importance from the whole interval.
  • Scope of the conclusion: random sampling lets us generalize to the populations; random assignment in a randomized experiment lets us conclude that the treatment caused the difference. Without random assignment, do not claim causation.
  • Do not compare two separate intervals (for p1 and p2) to judge a difference. Build one interval for the difference.

Example 5 — Does the interval show a difference?

(a) In Example 3, the 95% interval for the difference in the proportions of adults who sleep better (app minus no app) is (0.026, 0.214). (b) Independent random samples of 150 students from School A and 180 from School B are asked whether they walk to school; 48 and 63 walk. A 95% interval for pA − pB is (−0.132, 0.072). What does each interval say?

(a) All plausible values are positive and 0 is not in the interval, so there is convincing evidence that the proportion of adults who sleep better is greater for those who use the app, by roughly 3 to 21 percentage points. (Because these were random samples, not a randomized experiment, we cannot conclude the app caused the improvement.)

(b) The interval (−0.132, 0.072) contains 0, so “no difference” is plausible: there is not convincing evidence that the proportions of students who walk differ between the two schools. We do not conclude the proportions are equal; the data are simply consistent with no difference.

Example 6 — Confidence level, sample size and a claim

A 95% interval for p1 − p2 is (0.03, 0.15). (a) What are the point estimate and the margin of error? (b) A researcher claims the difference is more than 0.05. Is that claim justified? (c) What would happen to the margin of error if both samples were four times as large?

(a) Point estimate = (0.03 + 0.15)/2 = 0.09; ME = (0.15 − 0.03)/2 = 0.06.

(b) No. Values from 0.03 to 0.05 are plausible, so the interval does not provide convincing evidence that the difference exceeds 0.05 (it does show that the difference is positive).

(c) SE is divided by √4 = 2, so the margin of error becomes 0.06/2 = 0.03.

1. Base case at 95%: what percentage of the intervals capture the true difference? What percentage contain 0?

About 95% capture the true difference (0.10). Since the true difference is not 0, intervals that contain 0 are the ones where we would fail to detect it: with samples of 150, roughly half of the intervals contain 0, because the difference is not much larger than the margin of error.

2. Increase both sample sizes to 600. What changes?

The intervals are about half as wide and the capture rate is still about 95%. Far fewer intervals contain 0, so we detect the difference much more often.

3. Set p1 = p2 (“No real difference”). How many intervals contain 0?

About 95% of them: the true difference is 0, so “contains 0” is the same as “captures the true difference”. The other 5% would wrongly suggest a difference.

4. Change the confidence level to 99%. What happens?

Fewer intervals miss the true difference (about 1%), but each interval is wider, and more of them contain 0.
