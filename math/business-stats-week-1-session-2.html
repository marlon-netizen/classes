Why and how we sample

A census is often impossible, too slow or too expensive, so we study a sample. To generalise, the sample must be representative; the safest way is to let chance, not the researcher’s choice, decide who is included.

Target population
The group you want to learn about (all customers).
Sampling frame
The actual list or mechanism from which units are drawn (e.g. the CRM list). The frame never matches the target population exactly.
Sample
The units finally selected.
Respondents
The part of the sample that actually answers or can be measured.
Target population → frame → sample → respondents

Each step can lose or distort information. Always check who is missing at every step.

Sampling methods

Probability sampling (chance decides; known selection probabilities)

Probability sampling
MethodHowStrengthsWatch out
Simple randomEvery unit and every group of size n equally likely (random numbers, draw from a hat)Unbiased, easy to analyseNeeds a complete frame; may miss small groups
SystematicInterval k = N/n; random start 1…k; then every k-th unitEasy to run on a list or production lineBias if the list has a repeating pattern that matches k
StratifiedSplit into homogeneous strata (region, segment); random sample within every stratum (proportional or equal)More precise; guarantees each group is representedNeeds a variable to stratify on
ClusterRandomly choose whole clusters (stores, classes); measure everyone insideCheap when units are spread outLess precise if clusters differ from each other
MultistageCombine the above in stages (regions → stores → customers)Practical for large populationsMore complex analysis

Non-probability sampling (selection not by chance: cannot measure sampling error)

Use for exploration, not for inference
MethodHowRisk
ConvenienceWhoever is easiest to reachStrong selection bias
Voluntary responsePeople choose to respond (web polls, call-ins)Over-represents strong opinions
Judgment (purposive)Researcher picks “typical” unitsDepends on the researcher’s judgment
QuotaFill set numbers per group, but chosen non-randomlyLooks like stratified, is not random
SnowballRespondents recruit othersNarrow, connected groups only

Try it: draw samples and watch the regions

Stratified vs cluster – the one-line difference

Stratified: sample some units from every group. Cluster: sample every unit from some groups.

Interval and allocation calculations

k = NnSystematic sampling interval (round down if not an integer)
nh = n × NhNProportional allocation: sample size for stratum h

Worked example · systematic

An auditor must examine n = 60 of N = 1,200 invoices with a systematic sample and a random start of 13.

  1. k = N / n = 1,200 / 60 = 20.
  2. Selected invoices: 13, 33, 53, …
  3. The 10th invoice is 13 + 9 × 20 = 193.
Answer: k = 20; the 10th selected invoice is #193.

Worked example · stratified

A bank has 600 customers: 300 urban, 200 suburban, 100 rural. It needs n = 60 with proportional allocation.

  1. Urban: 60 × 300/600 = 30.
  2. Suburban: 60 × 200/600 = 20.
  3. Rural: 60 × 100/600 = 10.
  4. Check: 30 + 20 + 10 = 60.
Answer: 30 urban, 20 suburban, 10 rural.

Sampling and survey error

Total error = sampling error + non-sampling errors.

The error family
ErrorWhat it isRemedy
Sampling errorNatural chance difference between a statistic and the parameter, because we only see part of the population. Measurable; shrinks as n grows (about 1/√n).Larger n, better design (stratify)
Coverage errorThe frame omits (or wrongly includes) members of the target populationUpdate and check the frame
Non-response errorSelected units do not respond and non-respondents differ from respondentsFollow-ups, incentives, short surveys, compare respondents with known population
Measurement errorWrong answers recorded: poor wording, interviewer effects, social-desirability, faulty instrumentsPilot test, neutral wording, calibration
Processing errorMistakes in coding, data entry or analysisValidation rules, double-entry checks
Bigger is not always better

A huge biased sample is still biased. A famous example: a magazine’s 1936 US election poll of over two million people predicted the wrong winner because its frame (car and phone owners) left out many voters. Fixing non-sampling errors needs better design, not more data.

Good questionnaire design

  • Neutral, not leading (“Don’t you agree…”).
  • One idea per question, avoid double-barrelled questions (“How satisfied are you with price and quality?”).
  • Specific time frame (“in the last 30 days”) and clear definitions.
  • Balanced answer options, covering all possibilities without overlap.
  • Pilot test on a few people first.

Sources of data

  • Primary (collected by you for this purpose: surveys, experiments, observation) vs secondary (collected by someone else: government statistics, industry reports, company records).
  • Observational studies record what happens; experiments assign treatments (A/B test) and can support causal claims.
  • Internal data (sales, CRM, web logs) vs external data (market data, census).

Cleaning workflow

  1. Keep an untouched copy of the raw data.
  2. Look at it: sort, filter, summarise each column.
  3. Fix structure (one row per case, one column per variable, headers in row 1).
  4. Remove duplicates; standardise labels, dates and units.
  5. Handle missing and invalid values (investigate, correct, or flag; never silently invent).
  6. Document every change.

Excel habits that protect your analysis
  • One header row, no blank rows or columns, no merged cells inside the data.
  • One value per cell; units in the header (“Spend ($)”), not in the cell.
  • Never type over raw data; do cleaning in copies or new columns.
  • =RAND() changes every time the sheet recalculates: paste as values once you have your sample.

More worked examples

Worked example · naming the method

A hotel chain lists its 40 hotels by region (North 10, South 15, East 15). It randomly chooses 4, 6 and 6 hotels respectively, then surveys all guests present on one weekend in each chosen hotel.

  1. Hotels are sampled within every region → regions are strata (proportional: 4/10, 6/15, 6/15 each 40 %).
  2. Everyone in a chosen hotel is surveyed → hotels are clusters.
  3. Both ideas appear: this is multistage sampling (stratified selection of clusters).
Answer: Multistage: stratified at the first stage, cluster sampling of hotels.

Worked example · which error?

A phone survey on app satisfaction reaches only customers who answer calls at 10 am on weekdays; 70 % of those called hang up.

  1. Those reached differ from those not reached (working customers are missing) → non-response / coverage bias.
  2. This is not sampling error: increasing n will not repair it.
Answer: Non-response (and coverage) error.
