Hypothesis Tests for a Population Proportion

A confidence interval estimates a population proportion. A significance test evaluates a claim about it: is the sample result far enough from the claimed value that random chance is not a plausible explanation? Lessons 3.5–3.8 take you from setting up the hypotheses, to interpreting the P-value, to concluding, to understanding the errors that can occur and the power of a test.

Objectives

  • Perform a significance test to evaluate a claim about a population proportion.
  • Interpret Type I and Type II errors and their possible consequences in context.
  • Understand the relationship among the significance level, the probability of a Type II error, and power.
z = p − p0√p0(1 − p0) / n The test statistic for H0: p = p0. Everything in the denominator uses the claimed value p0.

Lesson 3.5 — Setting up a significance test

LESSON 3.5

The logic of a significance test

Closely related to estimating a population proportion is testing a claim about it. A travel agency might compute an interval estimate for the proportion of sunny days in the Virgin Islands or, instead, test a tourist bureau’s claim about the proportion of sunny days.

The general procedure: choose a specific hypothesis to be tested, the null hypothesis H0; pick an appropriate random sample; and use the sample to judge how likely H0 is. If the sample statistic is far enough away from the claimed population parameter, there is sufficient evidence to reject H0. We attempt to show that the null hypothesis is unacceptable by showing that it is improbable.

  • The sample statistic almost never equals the claimed parameter. Either it differs by random chance or the claimed parameter is wrong.
  • If we can eliminate random chance as a plausible explanation, we have convincing evidence that the claimed parameter is wrong.
LESSON 3.5

Null and alternative hypotheses

  • The null hypothesis H0 is an equality about the population proportion, for example H0: p = 0.37.
  • The alternative hypothesis Ha is a strict inequality: Ha: p < 0.37, or Ha: p > 0.37, or Ha: p ≠ 0.37.
  • Hypotheses are about the parameter p, never about the statistic p. Define p in context: “p = the proportion of all [population] who [success]”.
  • Direction: the direction of Ha comes from the research question (“more than”, “less than”), decided before seeing the data. If the question does not suggest a direction, use a two-sided test (≠).
Choosing Ha
Wording of the claim or questionHaType of test
“more than”, “greater”, “higher”, “exceeds”p > p0one-sided (right tail)
“less than”, “lower”, “decreased”p < p0one-sided (left tail)
“different from”, “changed”, “not fair”p ≠ p0two-sided (both tails)
LESSON 3.5

Significance level and conditions

Small P-values indicate that the observed result would be unusual if H0 were true. How small is “small”? The significance level α is a predetermined boundary used to decide whether a P-value is small or not. Common choices are α = 0.05, 0.01 and 0.10. A smaller α demands stronger evidence before rejecting H0.

The procedure is the one-sample z-test for a population proportion (calculator: 1-PropZTest). Because the P-value is a conditional probability calculated assuming H0: p = p0 is true, we use the claimed proportion p0 both in checking Normality and in the standard deviation:

σp = √p0(1 − p0) / n
  • Independence: random sample (or randomized experiment) and the 10% rule, n ≤ 0.10N.
  • Normality (large counts): np0 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p0) ≥ 10.
Test vs interval: a test uses p0 (we assume H0 is true). A confidence interval uses p in the standard error.

Lesson 3.6 — Interpreting the P-value

LESSON 3.6

What the P-value measures

The strength of the sample statistic is gauged through its associated P-value: the probability of obtaining a sample statistic as extreme as (or more extreme than) the one obtained, assuming the null hypothesis is true. The smaller the P-value, the more significant the difference between the null hypothesis and the sample results.

P-value = P(a result at least this extreme | H0 is true)
  • The P-value is a conditional probability: it assumes H0 is true. Under that assumption it measures how likely it is to get evidence for Ha as strong as or stronger than the observed evidence by chance alone.
  • It is not the probability that H0 is true, and 1 − P-value is not the probability that Ha is true.
  • Smaller P-value → stronger, more convincing statistical evidence for Ha.

Template for interpreting a P-value: “Assuming that p = p0 [in context], the probability of getting a sample proportion as extreme as (or more extreme than) [the observed value] by chance alone is [P-value].”

Lesson 3.7 — Carrying out the test and concluding

LESSON 3.7

Decision rule and conclusion

Decision rule
If…DecisionMeaning
P-value ≤ αReject H0convincing statistical evidence for Ha
P-value > αFail to reject H0not convincing statistical evidence for Ha
Be careful! When we do not reject the null hypothesis we never “accept” it. We only say that we do not have sufficient evidence to reject it.

Conclusion in context, linked to the P-value: “Since the P-value (___) is [less than / greater than] α = ___, we [reject / fail to reject] H0. There [is / is not] convincing evidence that [Ha in context].”

  1. Parameter: define p in context.
  2. Hypotheses: H0: p = p0 and Ha (<, > or ≠), and the significance level α.
  3. Procedure and checks: one-sample z-test for a proportion; independence (random, 10%); Normality (np0 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p0) ≥ 10).
  4. Mechanics: compute p, z and the P-value (or use 1-PropZTest).
  5. Conclusion in context with linkage to the P-value and α.

Connection to intervals: a value p0 that lies inside a (1 − α) confidence interval is plausible, so a two-sided test at level α will generally fail to reject it; a value outside the interval will generally be rejected.

Example 1 — Which expression gives the P-value?

A research dermatologist believes that cancers of the head and neck occur most often on the left side (the side next to a window when driving). In a review of 565 cases, 305 occurred on the left side. What is the resulting P-value?

H0: p = 0.5 and Ha: p > 0.5 (one-sided: the belief is about the left side more often). p = 305/565 ≈ 0.54.

σp = √((0.5)(1 − 0.5)/565) = √((0.5)(0.5)/565) ≈ 0.0210 (note: the claimed 0.5 is used).

z = (p − p0)/σ = (0.54 − 0.5)/√((0.5)(0.5)/565) ≈ 1.89 (using 305/565 exactly).

P-value = P(z > 0.54 − 0.50√(0.5)(0.5)/565) ≈ 0.0292.

The right choice is the one-sided expression that uses p0 = 0.5 in the standard deviation: P(z > (0.54 − 0.50)/√((0.5)(0.5)/565)). Doubling the tail would be wrong (not two-sided), and using (0.54)(0.46) would be wrong (that is a standard error for an interval, not a test).

Example 2 — A complete test, concluded at two significance levels

A union spokesperson claims that 75% of union members will support a strike if their basic demands are not met. A company negotiator believes the true percentage is lower and runs a hypothesis test. What is the conclusion if 87 out of a simple random sample of 125 union members say they will strike? (Give conclusions at the 0.05 and the 0.10 significance levels.) What error might have been committed?

Parameter: p = the proportion of all union members who will support a strike if their basic demands are not met.

Hypotheses: H0: p = 0.75 and Ha: p < 0.75.

Procedure: one-sample z-test for a population proportion.

Checks: independence: it is an SRS, and we assume 125 is less than 10% of all union members. Normality: np0 = 125(0.75) = 93.75 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p0) = 125(0.25) = 31.25 ≥ 10.

Mechanics: p = 87/125 = 0.696; 1-PropZTest gives z = −1.394 and P = 0.0816.

Conclusion:

  • At α = 0.05: since 0.0816 > 0.05 we fail to reject H0. There is not convincing evidence at the 5% significance level that the true percentage of union members who support a strike is less than 75%.
  • At α = 0.10: since 0.0816 < 0.10 we reject H0. There is convincing evidence at the 10% significance level that the true percentage who support a strike is less than 75%.

Possible errors: if H0 is not rejected (α = 0.05) a false H0 may have been missed: a Type II error; the union might call a strike thinking it has greater support than it actually has. If H0 is rejected (α = 0.10), a true H0 may have been rejected: a Type I error; the union might decide not to call a strike thinking it lacks support when it actually has it.

Example 3 — A two-sided test

A company claims that 20% of its snack bags contain a prize. A consumer group opens a random sample of 400 bags (far fewer than 10% of all bags) and finds 64 prizes. Is there convincing evidence that the true proportion is different from 0.20? Use α = 0.05.

Parameter: p = the proportion of all bags that contain a prize. Hypotheses: H0: p = 0.20, Ha: p ≠ 0.20 (no direction suggested, so two-sided).

Checks: random sample, 400 < 10% of all bags; np0 = 80 ≥ 10 and n(1 − p0) = 320 ≥ 10.

Mechanics: p = 64/400 = 0.16; σ = √(0.2 · 0.8/400) = 0.02; z = (0.16 − 0.20)/0.02 = −2.00; P-value = 2 · P(Z < −2.00) = 2(0.0228) = 0.0455.

Conclusion: since 0.0455 < 0.05 we reject H0. There is convincing evidence at the 5% level that the true proportion of bags with a prize is different from 0.20. (At α = 0.01 we would fail to reject.)

Lesson 3.8 — Errors, significance level and power

LESSON 3.8

Type I and Type II errors

There are two ways a test can go wrong: mistakenly rejecting a true null hypothesis, or mistakenly failing to reject a false one.

Decision vs population truth
H0 is trueH0 is false
Reject H0Type I error
(probability α)		Correct decision
(probability 1 − β = power)
Fail to reject H0Correct decision
(probability 1 − α)		Type II error
(probability β)
  • The α-risk, also called the significance level, is the probability of a Type I error (rejecting a true H0).
  • A Type II error (failing to reject a false H0) has probability β. β depends on which alternative value of p is true.
  • In context: always describe each error in words and give a consequence. A Type I error is a “false positive” (concluding an effect exists when it does not); a Type II error is a “false negative” (missing a real effect).

Courtroom illustration: H0: the person is innocent. A Type I error is finding an innocent person guilty; a Type II error is failing to convict a guilty person. Criminal trials try to minimize Type I errors by demanding unanimous guilty verdicts; many civil suits accept simple majority verdicts, minimizing Type II errors.

LESSON 3.8

Power

The power of a test against a specific alternative value of p is the probability that the test correctly rejects H0 when that alternative is true:

power = 1 − β = P(reject H0 | the specific alternative p is true) High power means the test is good at detecting a real difference. When p = p0, the “power” is just α.

What affects power (changing one factor at a time):

ChangeEffect on powerEffect on α and β
Increase the sample size npower increasesα unchanged; β decreases
Increase the significance level αpower increasesrisk of Type I error increases; β decreases
Decrease αpower decreasesType I risk decreases; β increases
True p farther from p0 (bigger effect)power increasesβ decreases
One-sided instead of two-sided test (true p in the direction of Ha)power increasessame α
Less variability in the datapower increasesβ decreases
Trade-off: with a fixed sample size, lowering α raises β (and lowers power). The only way to lower both at once is to increase n. Which error is worse depends on the context, so the significance level should be chosen with the consequences in mind.
LESSON 3.8

How to calculate β and power for a proportion

  1. Find the rejection boundary in terms of p using H0: critical value = p0 ± z*σ0, with σ0 = √(p0(1 − p0)/n). (Right-tailed: p0 + z*σ0; left-tailed: p0 − z*σ0; two-sided: both, using z* for α/2 in each tail.)
  2. Assume a specific true value p. The sampling distribution of p is now centered at that p with standard deviation σ = √(p(1 − p)/n).
  3. Power = the area of that distribution inside the rejection region. β = 1 − power = the area outside it.

The Power Sim tab shows these two distributions and shades α, β and power so you can see each effect.

Example 4 — Computing power

H0: p = 0.50 vs Ha: p > 0.50, a random sample of n = 100 and α = 0.05. Find the power against the alternative p = 0.60, and β.

σ0 = √(0.5 · 0.5/100) = 0.05, so the test rejects H0 when p > 0.50 + 1.645(0.05) = 0.5822.

If p = 0.60, then σ = √(0.6 · 0.4/100) = 0.0490.

Power = P(p > 0.5822) = P(Z > (0.5822 − 0.60)/0.0490) = P(Z > -0.362) = 0.641.

β = 1 − 0.641 = 0.359.

Interpretation: if the true proportion is 0.60, this test would reject the false null hypothesis about 64% of the time, and it would commit a Type II error about 36% of the time.

Example 5 — What changes the power?

Using Example 4 as a base (true p = 0.60), how does the power change if (a) the sample size is 400, (b) α = 0.01?

(a) n = 400: σ0 = 0.025, critical value = 0.50 + 1.645(0.025) = 0.5411, σ = 0.0245; power = P(Z > (0.5411 − 0.60)/0.0245) = 0.992. A much larger power, with the same α.

(b) α = 0.01 (z* = 2.326), n = 100: critical value = 0.50 + 2.326(0.05) = 0.6163; power = P(Z > (0.6163 − 0.60)/0.0490) = 0.370. A smaller α lowers the power (and the Type I error risk).

Example 6 — Errors in context

A manufacturer tests H0: p = 0.02 vs Ha: p > 0.02 for the proportion of defective parts. Describe a Type I error, a Type II error, and a consequence of each.

Type I error: concluding the defect rate is higher than 2% when it is actually 2%. Consequence: the production line may be stopped and repaired unnecessarily, costing time and money.

Type II error: failing to find evidence that the defect rate is higher than 2% when it really is higher. Consequence: defective parts continue to be produced and shipped to customers.

If shipping defective parts is the more serious problem, the manufacturer may choose a larger α (for example 0.10) to reduce β and increase power.

Explore (click to check your idea)

1. Use the base case (p0 = 0.50, true p = 0.60, n = 100, α = 0.05). What are β and the power? Then run 1000 tests.

Power ≈ 0.64 and β ≈ 0.36. About 64% of the 1000 simulated tests reject the false H0; about 36% make a Type II error.

2. Increase n from 100 to 400. What happens to α, β and power?

α stays at 0.05. The two curves become narrower and separate, so β drops and the power rises to about 0.99.

3. Change α from 0.05 to 0.01. What happens?

The rejection region shrinks. α falls to 0.01, but β rises and the power falls to about 0.37: the trade-off between Type I and Type II errors.

4. Set the true p equal to p0 and run 1000 tests. What fraction rejects?

About α (around 5% for α = 0.05): if H0 is true, every rejection is a Type I error, and the “power” equals α.

5. Compare the right-tailed test with the two-sided test (true p = 0.60). Which has more power, and why?

The right-tailed test, because it puts all of α in the tail where the true value lies. The two-sided test spends α/2 on the other tail, so its critical value is farther from p0.
